# Sideband: Q-Codes — The Shorthand Language of Amateur Radio

**Tags:** `[F]` `[I]` `[FL]`
**RSGB Refs:** 8A1, 8B1
**Cross-refs:** Operating Procedures, Digital Modes
**Series:** Sideband — Topic Snippets for RF-Hub

---

Q-codes are three-letter abbreviations beginning with Q, originally developed for commercial radiotelegraphy in the early 1900s. They let operators exchange standard messages quickly — regardless of language. Amateur radio adopted a subset of these codes, and they remain in daily use on HF, VHF, and UHF.

Each Q-code has two meanings: as a **statement** and as a **question**. Adding a question mark (or in CW, sending the prosign `?`) turns the statement into its corresponding question.

---

## The Essential Q-Codes

These are the codes you'll hear — and use — most often on the bands. Every Foundation licence holder should recognise them.

### Station Identity & Location

| Code | As a Statement | As a Question |
|------|---------------|---------------|
| **QRA** | The name of my station is... | What is the name of your station? |
| **QRZ** | You are being called by... | Who is calling me? |
| **QTH** | My location is... | What is your location? |
| **QRB** | The distance between us is... | How far are you from my station? |
| **QTR** | The exact time is... | What is the exact time? |

### Signal Reports & Readability

| Code | As a Statement | As a Question |
|------|---------------|---------------|
| **QRK** | Your signal readability is... (1–5) | What is the readability of my signals? |
| **QSB** | Your signals are fading | Are my signals fading? |
| **QRM** | I am being interfered with (man-made) | Are you being interfered with? |
| **QRN** | I am troubled by static (natural noise) | Are you troubled by static? |

> **QRM vs QRN:** QRM is interference from other stations or electrical equipment — things humans made. QRN is natural noise: thunderstorms, atmospheric static, space weather. The distinction matters when troubleshooting reception problems.

### Operating Controls

| Code | As a Statement | As a Question |
|------|---------------|---------------|
| **QRL** | This frequency is in use | Is this frequency in use? |
| **QRV** | I am ready | Are you ready? |
| **QRX** | Stand by / I will call you again at... | Shall I stand by? |
| **QRT** | I am shutting down / stop sending | Shall I stop sending? |
| **QSY** | Change to frequency... | Shall I change frequency? |

> **QRL — The Polite First Step:** Before calling CQ on any frequency, always send "QRL?" first. If someone responds "QRL" (yes, busy), move on. Calling over an existing QSO is poor operating practice.

### Power & Speed

| Code | As a Statement | As a Question |
|------|---------------|---------------|
| **QRO** | Increase power (running high power) | Shall I increase power? |
| **QRP** | Decrease power (running low power) | Shall I decrease power? |
| **QRS** | Send more slowly | Shall I send more slowly? |
| **QRG** | Your exact frequency is... | Will you tell me my exact frequency? |

> **QRP Culture:** QRP has taken on a life of its own in amateur radio. QRP operators deliberately run very low power — typically 5 W or less on CW, 10 W on SSB — to see how far they can communicate with minimal power. There are dedicated QRP contests, clubs (like the G-QRP Club in the UK), and purpose-built QRP transceivers. Working DX on 5 watts and a wire antenna is one of the most satisfying achievements in the hobby.

### Confirmation & Contact

| Code | As a Statement | As a Question |
|------|---------------|---------------|
| **QSL** | I confirm / I acknowledge receipt | Can you acknowledge receipt? |
| **QSO** | I can communicate with... directly | Can you communicate with... directly? |

> **QSL Cards:** The tradition of sending QSL cards — physical postcards confirming a contact — dates back to the 1920s. Today, electronic QSL systems like **Logbook of The World (LoTW)**, **eQSL**, and **QRZ.com** have largely replaced paper cards, though many operators still collect and display them. A QSL card confirms both stations made the contact — essential for award claims like DXCC and SOTA.

## Common Abbreviations & Prosigns

Q-codes don't exist in isolation. Amateur radio uses a rich set of abbreviations and prosigns alongside them. Here are the ones you'll encounter most:

| Code | Meaning | Context |
|------|---------|---------|
| **73** | Best wishes / regards | Universal sign-off. NOT "73s" — 73 already means best wisheS (plural). |
| **88** | Love and kisses | Traditional sign-off, less commonly used today |
| **CQ** | Calling any station | "Seek you" — general call inviting any station to respond |
| **CQ DX** | Calling distant stations | Seeking long-distance contacts specifically |
| **DE** | From (this is...) | Identifies the sending station: "DE G7ABC" |
| **DX** | Long distance | Any contact outside your own country (or sometimes DXCC entity) |
| **OM** | Old Man | Friendly term for a male operator (not age-related) |
| **YL** | Young Lady | Female operator (not age-related) |
| **XYL** | Ex-Young Lady | Wife (traditional, somewhat dated) |
| **HI** | Laughter | The CW equivalent of "ha ha" |
| **R** | Roger / received | Confirms message received |
| **K** | Go ahead / over | Inviting the other station to transmit |
| **SK** | End of contact | Final sign-off, conversation complete |
| **AR** | End of message | Message complete, but contact may continue |
| **BK** | Break | Quick interrupt, used in semi-duplex exchanges |
| **WX** | Weather | "WX here is sunny, 22°C" |
| **RIG** | Equipment | Your radio setup — "RIG is IC-7610" |
| **ANT** | Antenna | "ANT is a dipole at 10m" |
| **PSE** | Please | Abbreviation used in CW |
| **TU** | Thank you | Common CW courtesy |
| **FB** | Fine business | Means "great" or "excellent" |
| **SOTA** | Summits On The Air | Activating a summit for contacts |
| **POTA** | Parks On The Air | Activating a park for contacts |

## RST — The Signal Report System

When exchanging signal reports during a QSO, amateurs use the **RST system**:

| Letter | Meaning | Scale | Used On |
|--------|---------|-------|---------|
| **R** | Readability | 1–5 (1 = unreadable, 5 = perfectly readable) | All modes |
| **S** | Signal Strength | 1–9 (1 = barely perceptible, 9 = very strong) | All modes |
| **T** | Tone | 1–9 (1 = rough, 9 = pure tone) | CW only |

A report of **59** on SSB means "perfectly readable, very strong signal." On CW, **599** adds "pure tone." In practice, many operators default to giving 59/599 unless the signal is genuinely poor — a habit worth resisting, as honest reports are more useful.

## UK Licence Considerations

All Q-codes listed here are permitted at **Foundation** level and above. There are no Q-codes restricted by licence class — they're operating conventions, not regulatory requirements.

However, Foundation licence holders should be aware:
- **QRO** is limited by your licence power level (25 W for Foundation)
- **QSY** — you can only move to frequencies your licence class permits
- **QSL** via LoTW requires a valid callsign and may need your licence document for verification

## Quick Reference — The Top 10

If you remember nothing else, know these ten:

| Code | What It Means | When You'll Use It |
|------|--------------|-------------------|
| QRZ? | Who is calling me? | When you hear your callsign but missed who called |
| QTH | My location is... | Every contact includes a location exchange |
| QSL | Confirmed / acknowledged | Confirming receipt of information |
| QSO | A radio contact | "Had a nice QSO on 40m today" |
| QSY | Change frequency | Moving to avoid interference or find a clear channel |
| QRL? | Is this frequency in use? | Always ask before calling CQ |
| QRP | Low power | Running 5 W or less |
| QRT | Shutting down | "Going QRT for the night, 73" |
| QRV | Ready | "QRV on 145.500" — ready and listening |
| 73 | Best wishes | End of every contact |
