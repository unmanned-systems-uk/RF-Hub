## Part 1: What a Filter Does

A filter is a circuit that treats different frequencies differently — it lets some through with little loss and attenuates (weakens) others, based purely on frequency. That's the whole idea: **frequency-selective attenuation.** Everything else in this page is detail on top of that one sentence.

## Part 2: The Four Types — Three Ways to See Each One

The exam shows filters as **amplitude-vs-frequency response curves**. In the real world you also meet them as their effect on a **spectrum** (which frequency components are present) and on a **waveform** (what the signal looks like on a scope). Seeing all three side by side builds real intuition, not just plot-recognition.

### Low-pass

<img src="/assets/images/filters/filter-response-lowpass.svg" alt="Low-pass filter amplitude response: flat passband, falls away above the cut-off frequency fc" width="700" height="376" loading="lazy">

Passes DC and low frequencies, attenuates high frequencies above the cut-off fc.

**Worked example — a square wave through a low-pass filter (waveform view):** A square wave is not "one frequency" — Fourier analysis shows it's built from a fundamental sine wave plus an infinite series of odd harmonics (3rd, 5th, 7th...), added together in the right proportions to build the sharp edges and flat tops. A low-pass filter with a cut-off just above the fundamental removes most of those harmonics. What's left is close to a pure sine wave at the fundamental frequency — the sharp corners and flat top of the square wave round off, because the harmonics that built those sharp features are gone.

> [INFO] **Key idea:** removing harmonics from a waveform always rounds off its sharp features. This is exactly why a transmitter's **harmonic low-pass filter** cleans up a distorted output stage — see the spectrum example below.

**Worked example — TX harmonics through the station low-pass filter (spectrum view):**

<img src="/assets/images/filters/spectrum-before-after-harmonic-lpf.svg" alt="Spectrum before and after a transmitter harmonic low-pass filter: before shows the fundamental plus several harmonics above the cut-off, after shows only the fundamental with harmonics suppressed" width="700" height="263" loading="lazy">

Before the filter, a transmitter's PA output contains the wanted fundamental (f0) plus unwanted harmonics (2f0, 3f0, 4f0...) generated by any non-linearity in the amplifier. The low-pass filter's cut-off sits just above f0, so the fundamental passes almost unchanged while the harmonics — all at higher frequencies — are heavily attenuated. This is the filter behind the "no spurious emissions" requirement in your licence.

### High-pass

<img src="/assets/images/filters/filter-response-highpass.svg" alt="High-pass filter amplitude response: falls away below the cut-off frequency fc, flat passband above" width="700" height="376" loading="lazy">

The mirror image of low-pass: attenuates below fc, passes above.

### Band-pass

<img src="/assets/images/filters/filter-response-bandpass.svg" alt="Band-pass filter amplitude response: a hump between two cut-off frequencies, flat top at f0, attenuated both sides, bandwidth marked" width="700" height="376" loading="lazy">

Passes a range of frequencies around a centre frequency f0, attenuates both above and below. Every IF filter and RF preselector in a superhet is a band-pass filter.

### Band-stop / notch

<img src="/assets/images/filters/filter-response-bandstop.svg" alt="Band-stop (notch) filter amplitude response: a narrow dip cut out of an otherwise flat response, centred on f0" width="700" height="376" loading="lazy">

The mirror image of band-pass: a narrow dip removed from an otherwise flat response, rather than a hump added to one.

**Worked example — a heterodyne whistle through an audio notch (spectrum view):**

<img src="/assets/images/filters/spectrum-before-after-notch.svg" alt="Spectrum before and after a notch filter: before shows a broadband noise floor plus one tall interfering tone, after shows the same noise floor with the tone removed" width="700" height="263" loading="lazy">

A steady heterodyne whistle (a single interfering carrier beating against your receiver) shows up as one tall, narrow spike in the audio spectrum, sitting on top of the wanted noise floor. A notch filter tuned exactly to that spike removes it while leaving everything else — including the wanted signal's own noise floor — essentially untouched. Modern DSP receivers can find and track this automatically ("auto-notch").

## Part 3: LC Series vs Parallel — In Depth

Any of the four filter types above can be built from an LC (inductor + capacitor) resonant circuit — the specific behaviour depends on **how** the L and C are combined and **where** that combination sits in the circuit.

<img src="/assets/images/filters/lc-placement-4way.svg" alt="Four LC placements and their filter behaviour: series LC in-line gives band-pass, series LC shunt to ground gives notch, parallel LC in-line gives notch, parallel LC shunt gives band-pass" width="700" height="403" loading="lazy">

| LC type | At resonance (f0) | RSGB name | In-line (series path) | As a shunt (to ground) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Series LC** | **Minimum** impedance, maximum current | "Acceptor" | Passes f0 → **band-pass** | Shorts f0 to ground → **notch** (series-resonant trap) |
| **Parallel LC** | **Maximum** impedance, minimum line current, large *circulating* current in the tank | "Rejector" | Blocks f0 → **notch** (e.g. antenna trap) | Passes f0 to the output → **band-pass** (IF tank, tuned amplifier load) |

**Why "acceptor" and "rejector"?** A series LC at resonance is the lowest-impedance path it will ever present — it "accepts" current at that one frequency more readily than any other. A parallel LC at resonance is the highest-impedance it will ever present — it "rejects" current flow through itself at that frequency (while a large current still circulates internally, sloshing between L and C).

**Impedance-vs-frequency view** — the exam sometimes shows impedance rather than amplitude:

<img src="/assets/images/filters/lc-impedance-series.svg" alt="Series LC impedance vs frequency: V-shaped dip to a minimum at f0" width="700" height="376" loading="lazy">
<img src="/assets/images/filters/lc-impedance-parallel.svg" alt="Parallel LC impedance vs frequency: sharp peak to a maximum at f0" width="700" height="376" loading="lazy">

Series = V-shaped dip at f0. Parallel = sharp peak at f0.

> [INFO] **Memory aid:** *"Series = Small impedance."* Get series right and parallel follows by elimination — it's the opposite in every column of the table above.

**Practical traps:** an antenna trap is a parallel LC placed in-line in the antenna element — at its resonant frequency it presents a high impedance, electrically shortening the antenna at that frequency while leaving it at full length below resonance. This is how a single trapped dipole covers two bands from one antenna — see the <a href="../study/intermediate/section-4-feeders-antennas.html" target="_blank">Int §4 — Feeders and Antennas</a> trapped dipole example.

## Part 5: Filters in Your Station

Which filters you own — and where they physically live — depends on your receiver's architecture. Every modern rig has a **harmonic low-pass filter** at the antenna output on transmit (required by your licence's spurious-emission conditions) and some form of selectivity on receive, but *how* that receive selectivity is achieved differs:

**Traditional superhet architecture** (analogue IF chain):
- **Roofing filter** — a wide band-pass filter early in the IF chain, protecting later, narrower filters from being overloaded by strong nearby signals.
- **Crystal / mechanical IF filter** — a narrow band-pass filter that sets the receiver's selectivity in the analogue domain. See the <a href="crystal-filter.html" target="_blank">Crystal Filters sideband</a> for how these are built from ladder and half-lattice crystal networks.

**Direct-sampling SDR architecture** (ADC close to the antenna, no analogue IF):
- **Preselector band-pass filter(s)** — analogue band-pass filtering ahead of the ADC, broad enough to cover the band(s) in use, mainly there for image/out-of-band rejection and to protect the ADC's dynamic range rather than to set final selectivity.
- **Anti-alias filter** — a low-pass (or band-pass) filter ahead of the ADC, preventing frequencies above the sampling system's Nyquist limit from aliasing into the wanted spectrum (see §2F's digital signals section).
- **Digital IF/DSP filtering** — once digitised, all fine selectivity (narrow CW/SSB filtering, notch, auto-notch) is done in software, not with physical analogue filters.

Both architectures still need the TX **harmonic low-pass filter**, and both can implement an **audio/DSP notch & auto-notch** (band-stop, removing a single interfering tone from receive audio, as in the whistle example above) — that stage is software in both cases on a modern rig.

Elsewhere in the amateur world, a **TVI high-pass filter** is fitted at a TV or other victim receiver's aerial input (not at your station) to block amateur HF energy while passing the TV channel's higher frequencies. A **diplexer** splits (or combines) two different frequency bands onto separate ports using a pair of filters (e.g. HF and VHF sharing one antenna feed); a **duplexer** lets a single antenna transmit and receive simultaneously on different frequencies (e.g. an FM repeater) by combining a band-pass and band-reject filter pair to isolate TX from RX.

Exactly which of the above your own rig uses, and where the line between "analogue" and "digital" falls, is architecture-specific and changes between models and even firmware revisions — check your rig's manual or block diagram rather than assuming.

## Part 6: Reading a Filter Spec Sheet

A real filter's datasheet or spec table uses a small standard vocabulary:

- **-3 dB bandwidth:** the width of the passband measured at the points where response has fallen 3 dB (about 70.7% of peak voltage, half of peak power) below maximum. The everyday "bandwidth" figure.
- **Shape factor:** the ratio of the -60 dB bandwidth to the -6 dB bandwidth. A shape factor close to 1.0 means a very steep-sided ("brick wall") filter; a large shape factor means a gentle roll-off. Crystal filters can achieve shape factors below 2:1; simple LC filters are typically much worse.
- **Passband ripple:** small variations in response *within* the passband (not the roll-off at the edges). Some filter designs (e.g. Chebyshev) trade a small amount of ripple for a steeper roll-off than a ripple-free design (Butterworth) of the same complexity.
- **Stopband attenuation:** how many dB of attenuation the filter provides deep in the stopband, well away from the cut-off — this is what actually determines whether an unwanted signal is suppressed enough to matter.
- **Insertion loss:** how much the filter attenuates its *own* passband. Ideally 0 dB; every real filter loses a little signal even for wanted frequencies, due to component losses (a lower-Q inductor costs you here).
- **Steepness vs ringing / group delay:** a steeper filter (higher order, sharper skirts) generally has worse phase behaviour — it delays different frequencies within the passband by different amounts (group delay distortion) and can "ring" (produce a decaying oscillation) in response to a sudden edge in the signal. This is the practical trade-off behind "just make every filter as steep as possible" not being free: a very steep CW filter, for example, can sound like it's ringing on fast keying.
