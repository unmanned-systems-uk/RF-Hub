Most of the licensing conditions you need for everyday operating were covered at Foundation and Intermediate. The Full licence builds on that foundation in three main areas: **international operating privileges** (CEPT T/R 61-01), the **EMF compliance framework** (using the Ofcom calculator), and a deeper reading of **Schedule 1** including Primary/Secondary allocations, the 5 MHz conditions, EIRP limits, and callsign format. There is also new material on emission designators and bandwidth terminology.

The chapter is deliberately concise — the RSGB manual runs to only five pages, since many conditions are already familiar. However, the **schedule and the Notice of Co-ordination** both appear in the exam Reference Booklet (EX309) and their contents *are* examinable. When an exam question says "which answer can be found in the Reference Booklet?", the answer is usually in the schedule notes.

> [INFO] The Ofcom document *Amateur Radio Guidance* (OFW611) amplifies the licence and gives guidance on procedures and compliance. It is **not** itself examinable, but understanding it helps you interpret exam questions. A copy is provided in the Reference Booklet at the examination.

## 1A — How This Chapter Works

The Full Licence Manual references licence clauses throughout as **(N-n)**, meaning Condition N, clause n. For example, **(6-4)** is Condition 6, clause 4 (the purpose of amateur radio). You will see these references in exam questions — look up the answer in the Reference Booklet during the exam.

**Terminology note:** The word "Licensee" and specific terms in Initial Capitals have defined meanings in Condition 10 (Interpretation). When studying, check the exact meaning if a term seems unusual.

## 1B — Content of Messages (1C1)

### Plain Language and Permitted Codes

Messages from your station must only be addressed to other amateur stations in the UK or around the world. The licence restricts transmissions to self-training and leisure activities — commercial use is not permitted **(6-4)**, with an exception when assisting in disaster or local, national, or international emergency **(6-5)**.

Amateur messages must be in **plain language** — anyone listening must be able to understand the general content. Q-codes and standard abbreviations are explicitly permitted because they facilitate passing messages when radio conditions are poor or there are language difficulties. They do not obscure the message in the way that encryption would. The distinction matters in exam questions.

**Encryption** is not permitted in amateur transmissions except in a specific circumstance: when assisting a User Service (such as ambulance or mountain rescue) where the name of a casualty may need to be obscured, the licence allows an encrypted message or some other method of obscuring its real meaning **(6-19)**. The decision to use encryption in this context is that of the User Service, not the amateur.

> [INFO] **1C1:** Messages must be in plain language; Q-codes and abbreviations are permitted because they facilitate communication — they are not encryption. Encryption of content is not permitted. Exception: assisting a User Service where casualty identity must be protected **(6-19)**.

### Message Content Standards

The **Wireless Telegraphy (Content of Transmission) Regulations 1988** prohibit grossly offensive, indecent, or menacing messages. The **Communications Act 2003** provides the broader legal framework. You should not need to avoid these standards — but a breach could be grounds for licence revocation.

When working overseas stations, bear in mind that cultural norms differ. You should strive to avoid causing offence even when technically within UK law.

## 1C — Transmitter Standards and Undue Interference (1D1)

### Clean and Stable Transmissions

Condition 6, clauses 1 and 2, require that your equipment:
- Transmits in accordance with the licence
- Is designed, constructed, maintained, and used so it does **not** cause Undue Interference to any wireless telegraphy

The Wireless Telegraphy Act 2006 defines **Undue Interference** as persistent and harmful interference.

> [INFO] **1D1:** "Undue Interference" is defined in the WT Act 2006 as persistent and harmful. Your transmitter must not drift excessively, produce excess harmonics, or cause sustained interference to other users. You must test periodically to confirm compliance.

### Ofcom Inspection

A person authorised by Ofcom has the right to inspect your licence and equipment — either if an urgent situation exists (e.g. a complaint about excessive field strengths) or to verify compliance with the licence **(7-1)**. This may result from a neighbour complaint about TV interference or field strengths that exceed EMC directive limits.

You also have an obligation to **conduct periodic tests** to ensure your station is not causing Undue Interference, and to keep records of those tests. These records can prove you take compliance seriously if a complaint is escalated.

### Receiving Capability

The licence requires you to have equipment capable of **receiving messages on all frequencies and modes** used by your station **(see 1D1 syllabus wording)**. In practice this means you should be able to monitor your own transmission band while operating, and should carry out regular critical listening to check for frequency drift, excessive bandwidth, or spurious emissions.

## 1D — Remote Control Operation (1E1)

### What Remote Control Means

The licence permits various forms of **unattended and remote control** operation. Remote control means you are not physically at the transmitter but have a control link that:
- Is adequately secure
- Allows you to promptly turn off the transmitter **(6-10 and 6-10x)**
- Fails safely — loss of the link stops transmissions

You are not required to be at the transmitter, but you must be able to terminate transmissions promptly from wherever you are.

### The Control Link

The choice of link technology is yours. However, if the control link uses an amateur band, it **must operate above 30 MHz (6-10b)**. The normal licence conditions apply equally to the link and to the main transmitter — in particular, the link must **not be encrypted (6-10)**. This is because amateur transmissions — including the control signals — must remain in plain language. Security must be achieved by other means (authentication, secure access codes, physical security).

When using the internet as a remote control link, the licensee may be in another country while the transmitter remains in the UK. The licence terms and call sign remain the normal UK ones, because the radio transmission (which is what is licensed) originates in the UK.

### Remote Location Notice

You must **display a notice at the remote location** giving your Licence Number in case you need to be contacted **(6-10d)**. Only Ofcom can relate the licence number to your personal details, keeping them secure.

> [INFO] **1E1:** Remote control: any communications link may be used; if in an amateur band it must be above 30 MHz; the link must not be encrypted; display licence number notice at remote location **(6-10d)**; the licence terms apply to the link as to the main station.

## 1E — International Operation: CEPT T/R 61-01 and HAREC (1F1)

### CEPT and the HAREC Certificate

**CEPT** (Conference of European Posts and Telecommunications) Recommendation **T/R 61-01** introduced the concept of reciprocal amateur radio operation across signatory countries. It applies **only to holders of a Full (personal) licence** — it does not extend to club licences or reciprocal (bilateral bilateral) licences.

All UK Full licence holders are issued with a **HAREC** (Harmonised Amateur Radio Examination Certificate) by the RSGB on passing the Full exam. The HAREC is internationally recognised across the signatory countries as proof that you have passed an examination at the required standard.

> [WARNING] CEPT T/R 61-01 applies to Full licence (HAREC) holders **only**. Foundation and Intermediate licence holders do not hold a HAREC and cannot use T/R 61-01. This is a standing exam rule and appears regularly in the question bank.

### Using T/R 61-01 Abroad

T/R 61-01 covers **temporary, non-resident operation for up to 3 months** in about 50 CEPT and non-CEPT countries that have signed the recommendation. Under it you may:
- Set up your own station, or
- Use the station of another licensed amateur

When operating under T/R 61-01 you must:
- Have your UK licence available for inspection (carry a paper copy — don't rely on the internet)
- Have a copy of the host country's rules, obtainable from their administration before you travel
- **Identify with your home call sign prefixed by the host country code** — e.g. operating in France as a UK M0 holder: **F/M0ABC**
- Comply with the host country's licence conditions, which may prohibit things allowed in the UK or allow things not permitted here
- **Check HF Morse requirements** — some host countries require a Morse test as a condition of HF operation; T/R 61-01 does not override this

> [INFO] **1F1:** CEPT T/R 61-01 permits Full licence (HAREC) holders to operate temporarily abroad (up to 3 months) in ~50 signatory countries. You must identify as HOST/HOMECALL (e.g. F/M0ABC). Comply with host country rules. Carry licence + host rules paper copies.

### T/R 61-02 and the HAREC for Longer Stays

**T/R 61-02** introduced the HAREC framework. If you are staying in a signatory country for **more than 3 months** (beyond T/R 61-01's scope), you may use your HAREC to apply for a full licence from that country's administration, as if you had passed their examination.

### Reciprocal Agreements

**Bilateral reciprocal agreements** exist between individual countries. When in place, UK licensees may operate in the host country under the terms of the agreement, which may involve obtaining a temporary licence. Non-CEPT countries often use this route. Check before travelling — procedures and requirements vary and customs authorities may impound equipment without the correct paperwork.

## 1F — ITU Radio Regions (1F2)

The **International Telecommunication Union (ITU)** divides the world into three regions for the purpose of radio frequency allocation. The frequencies allocated to amateur radio vary between regions:

| Region | Countries |
|--------|-----------|
| **Region 1** | Europe, Africa, Russia, the Middle East, former Soviet republics |
| **Region 2** | The Americas (North, Central, South America and Caribbean) |
| **Region 3** | Asia and Oceania (China, Japan, Australia, India, South-East Asia, Pacific) |

The UK is in **Region 1**. When operating abroad under T/R 61-01 (§1E above), you must use **only frequencies that appear in both your UK licence schedule AND are allocated to amateur radio in the region where you are operating**. Do not assume that a frequency in your UK schedule is valid in Region 2 or 3 — the allocations differ.

> [INFO] **1F2:** The ITU divides the world into three regions. UK = Region 1. When operating abroad, use only frequencies listed in both your UK schedule AND the host country's regional allocation. Americas (R2) and Asia/Pacific (R3) have different amateur allocations from Region 1.

## 1G — EMF Compliance Assessment (1G1)

### The ICNIRP Framework

The amateur licence requires that the RF fields around your station **do not exceed the limits recommended by ICNIRP** (International Commission on Non-Ionising Radiation Protection) applicable to members of the general public **(Condition 9-1)**. The "general public" includes non-amateur family, guests, neighbours, and anyone in areas accessible to them.

Licensed amateurs are assumed to have the knowledge to look after themselves — the protection requirement is for those who do not. The boundary of the "general public zone" is **any location where the general public may or can reasonably be expected to be present**, which includes your neighbours' gardens and any public road or path adjacent to your antenna.

> [INFO] **1G1:** Your licence requires compliance with ICNIRP EMF limits for the general public **(Condition 9-1)**. You must know: the origin of the restrictions (ICNIRP), the meaning of "general public", the areas to protect, the assessment procedure, record-keeping requirements, and emergency exceptions.

### When an Assessment is Required

If the **EIRP is below 10 W averaged over any 6-minute period OR the instantaneous peak EIRP is below 100 W**, exclusion zones do not apply — no formal assessment is required for that operating scenario.

If you operate above those thresholds (which most Intermediate and Full licence holders will, given the 100 W Intermediate / 1000 W Full power limits), you **must carry out a compliance assessment** for each antenna installation.

### The Ofcom/RSGB Assessment Tool

Ofcom have produced a spreadsheet that calculates the **compliance distance** (also called the exclusion zone distance) and the required **horizontal and vertical separation** from the antenna to any location where the general public may be present. The RSGB produce a simplified "front page" version of the spreadsheet.

The spreadsheet inputs are:
- **Transmit mode** (SSB, FM, CW, data — affects average power via duty cycle)
- **Transmit % in 6 minutes** (e.g. 70% if you talk approximately 4 out of every 6 minutes)
- **Feeder loss** (reduces power reaching the antenna)
- **Antenna type and gain** (higher gain = higher EIRP in the main lobe)
- **Height of antenna** above ground level

The outputs are:
- **Reactive near-field zone** (typically 0.1–0.5 m for HF antennas)
- **Ofcom Compliance Distance** (horizontal distance from antenna base to nearest accessible area)
- **Vertical Separation required** (if people are below the antenna)
- **Horizontal Separation required**

**Example (from RSGB manual, Fig 1.1):** 100 W FM on 2 m, 6-element Yagi at 7 m height — Ofcom Compliance Distance 9.2 m, Vertical Separation 5.2 m, Horizontal Separation 7.6 m. This distance could easily extend outside a small urban garden, requiring either power reduction or access restriction.

> [WARNING] A high-gain beam antenna aimed at accessible areas at close range will have a **higher** compliance distance than a dipole at the same power. Directivity increases field strength in the main lobe. Do not assume a beam is "safer" because power is concentrated away from some directions.

### Record-keeping

You must **keep a record showing how you are complying** with EMF requirements **(Condition 9-6)**. This does not have to be complex — a printed copy of the Ofcom/RSGB spreadsheet with your station parameters filled in is sufficient. Update it whenever you change antenna, power level, or operating location.

### Emergency Exception

There is no requirement to comply with ICNIRP guidelines during a genuine emergency if you are **necessarily located** at the site of the emergency and are conducting communications essential to the emergency response. Pre-planned exercises and preparedness training at a distance from the emergency do not qualify.

### Mobile Operation

When operating mobile (in a vehicle), consider:
- **Proximity to pedestrians and vehicle occupants** — if temporarily stationary you may be close to members of the public
- **Non-amateur passengers** in your own vehicle
- A metal-bodied vehicle provides useful screening of occupants from RF fields — but you must be able to demonstrate this if challenged

## 1H — Schedule 1, Callsigns, and Operating Conditions (1H1)

### Schedule 1 and the Notice of Co-ordination

The **Schedule to the licence** (the frequency allocation table) and the **Notice of Co-ordination** both contain **examinable content**. At Full licence level you are expected to read these more carefully than at Foundation or Intermediate, checking the specific conditions attached to each band.

The schedule shows **Primary** and **Secondary** allocations. An amateur band with **Secondary status** must always give way to Primary users. You may not know the Primary user's identity, but you must move if asked — your equipment must allow QSY. A band with **Secondary status** does NOT mean you cannot use it; it means you do so on a non-interference basis.

**Example (exam-applicable):** At 1.81–1.83 MHz the schedule states: *"Primary. Available on the basis of non-interference to other services outside the UK or Crown Dependencies."* This means you must give way to any service outside the UK claiming primary status on those frequencies.

### The 5 MHz (60 m) Conditions

The 5 MHz allocation carries **specific conditions in Schedule note G** shared with the Ministry of Defence (MoD), who is the Primary user. Key constraints (verify against current schedule before operating):

- The allocation consists of individual **channels** (not a continuous band), varying in width from 3 kHz to 12.5 kHz
- Staying within your allocated channel is essential because the adjacent channel may be in use by the MoD
- There is a **generic EIRP limit of 200 W** in addition to the normal PEP limit **(Schedule note G I i)**
- At 100 W PEP you are limited to an antenna gain of **3 dBi (isotropic) or 0.85 dBd (half-wave dipole)**
  - At 5 MHz, a half-wave dipole is approximately **28.5 m (93.5 ft) long**
  - Antenna **height is limited to 20 m** above ground level **(Schedule note G II)**

> [WARNING] The 5 MHz band is not a normal HF band — it has tightly defined channel widths, MoD primary use, EIRP limits, and antenna height restrictions. Operating outside these conditions is a licence violation. [VERIFY] against current Ofcom schedule and Notice of Co-ordination before operating on 5 MHz.

### Identification Requirements (Full Level)

Identification at Full level is **more specific** than at Foundation/Intermediate:
- **On joining or leaving a net** and **every 15 minutes** you must give your callsign **(Schedule note G VI)**
- You must be either at your **main station address** or a **temporary location**, and if at a temporary location must give that location every **30 minutes (Schedule note G VIII)**
- You must have a **telephone** at the number you registered on the Ofcom licensing portal when you took out the licence **(Schedule note G IX)**; you must be immediately reachable if Ofcom needs to contact you
- Any **change of personal details** must be reported to Ofcom promptly **(2-2)**

### Callsign Format

UK amateur call signs follow the format:

```
G#naaa   or   M#naaa   or   2#naaa

Where:
  #  = optional Regional Secondary Locator (RSL) — a letter giving approximate location
  n  = a digit (0–9)
  aaa = three letters
```

The **Regional Secondary Locator (RSL)** is optional in the sense that it need not be used, but when it **is** used it **must be correct** for the location of that transmission **(6-23)**. Using an incorrect RSL is a licence condition breach.

| Prefix series | Licence class |
|---|---|
| **M0, M1, M5** | Full licence (current series) |
| **G (various)** | Full licence (older series — G3, G4, G7 etc.) |
| **M8, M9** | Intermediate licence (new series, 2023+) |
| **2E** | Intermediate licence (older series) |
| **M3, M6, M7** | Foundation licence |

> [INFO] **1H1:** At Full level you must know: Schedule note content (examinable); Primary/Secondary allocation meanings; 5 MHz channel conditions and EIRP/height limits; callsign prefixes by licence class (M0/M1/M5 = Full; M8/M9 = Intermediate; M3/M6/M7 = Foundation); RSL must be correct when used; ID every 15 minutes on nets; telephone registered with Ofcom.

> **See also:** <a href="../../sidebands/uk-callsigns-and-rsls.html" target="_blank">Sideband — UK Callsigns & Regional Secondary Locators</a> — complete prefix/RSL/suffix reference including worked examples and the legacy G-series map.

### Unattended Operation Types

The licence permits unattended operation beyond the basic remote control discussed in §1D. Permitted types **(all are examples of unattended operation)**:

| Type | Licence clause | Description |
|---|---|---|
| **Beacon** | (6-12) | Automated propagation beacon — typically sends an identifying callsign and tone on a fixed frequency for others to assess propagation |
| **Gateway** | (6-14) | Connects the amateur station to the internet or other non-amateur network — e.g. Echolink, D-STAR reflector |
| **Repeater** | (6-15) | Receives on one frequency and simultaneously re-transmits on another — extends the range of mobile and portable stations |
| **Data station** | (6-17) | A generic term for any data transmitter or receiver (packet radio, APRS digipeater, etc.) |

Requirements for each can be complex, particularly regarding transmit power and location. A **Notice of Co-ordination** may be required from Ofcom before activating a repeater or gateway, and a **separate callsign** obtained from the RSGB. Readers intending to run these facilities should consult the Ofcom Notice of Co-ordination document and RSGB guidance.

## 1I — Emission Designators and Bandwidth Terms

*This section expands beyond the RSGB Chapter 1 text, covering material required at Full licence level. Emission designator codes are defined in ITU Radio Regulations Appendix 1 — [VERIFY] specific codes against current ITU RR before citing in any regulatory context.*

### Where Does the System Come From?

Emission designators were born from an early 20th-century need to classify the rapidly expanding variety of radio signals crossing national borders. When the International Telecommunication Union codified its Radio Regulations in the 1920s and 1930s, it became clear that each country needed a common vocabulary for signal types — one that a Japanese regulator and a Chilean regulator could both apply to the same signal and reach the same classification. The result was Appendix 1 of the ITU Radio Regulations: a compact, machine-readable three-character code that describes any emission unambiguously.

The system was designed to be **universal and legally precise**. Informal mode names like "SSB", "CW", or "FT8" are conversational shorthand — useful between operators who share context, but imprecise in legal documents. "SSB" does not specify whether the carrier is suppressed or reduced, or whether the signal carries voice, data, or something else. The emission designator **J3E** leaves no room for ambiguity: single sideband, suppressed carrier, single analogue channel, telephony.

The ITU system is used today by everyone who works at the boundary between radio operation and regulation. National regulators including Ofcom and the US FCC cite designators in licence documents and enforcement actions. IARU regional band planners use them to define which signal types belong in which segment of a band. Exam boards test whether candidates understand what they are actually licensed to emit. Spectrum monitoring services record designators when logging interference sources. Amateurs encounter them directly in NoV applications and in the fine print of Schedule 1.

Understanding the system also helps decode your own logbook. The ADIF logging format's MODE field loosely follows the designator taxonomy — FT8 is logged as "FT8" because that is the commonly understood name, but the underlying signal is classified **J2D** (SSB-type carrier, single digital channel with audio sub-carrier, data). Knowing the system makes the decode table below something you can actually use, not just memorise.

### The ITU Emission Designator System

The ITU uses a standardised system to describe the type of emission produced by a station. An **emission designator** has three mandatory characters (sometimes preceded by an optional bandwidth figure in kHz/MHz/GHz):

```
[Optional bandwidth] + Character 1 + Character 2 + Character 3

Character 1 — Type of modulation:
  A = Amplitude modulation, double sideband (AM)
  F = Frequency modulation (FM)
  G = Phase modulation (PM)
  J = Single sideband, suppressed carrier (SSB)
  R = Single sideband, reduced carrier
  P = Unmodulated pulse
  K = Amplitude-modulated pulse
  (others exist — see ITU RR Appendix 1)

Character 2 — Nature of the modulating signal:
  0 = No modulating signal (unmodulated carrier)
  1 = Single channel of digital information, no sub-carrier
  2 = Single channel of digital information, with sub-carrier
  3 = Single channel of analogue information
  7 = Multiple channels of digital information
  8 = Multiple channels of analogue information
  9 = Composite (analogue + digital)

Character 3 — Type of information transmitted:
  A = Telegraphy (Morse code, for direct printing or visual use)
  B = Telegraphy, for automatic reception
  D = Data, telemetry, or telecommand
  E = Telephony (voice)
  F = Television (video)
  N = No information (carrier only)
  W = Combination of the above
```

### Common Amateur Emission Designators

| Designator | Decodes as | Amateur use |
|---|---|---|
| **A1A** | AM double sideband — single channel digital, no sub-carrier — Morse | CW (Morse code) |
| **A3E** | AM double sideband — single channel analogue — telephony | AM voice (broadcast-style voice) |
| **F3E** | FM — single channel analogue — telephony | FM voice (VHF/UHF repeaters and simplex) |
| **J3E** | SSB suppressed carrier — single channel analogue — telephony | SSB voice (HF standard mode) |
| **F1D** | FM — single channel digital, no sub-carrier — data | RTTY (FSK direct FM) |
| **J2D** | SSB suppressed carrier — digital with sub-carrier — data | AFSK digital modes on SSB (PSK31, WSPR via audio sub-carrier) |
| **G1D** | Phase modulation — single channel digital, no sub-carrier — data | PSK-based digital modes |
| **F2D** | FM — digital with sub-carrier — data | Audio FSK digital modes via FM transmitter |

> [INFO] Key designators: **A1A** = CW, **F3E** = FM voice, **J3E** = SSB voice, **A3E** = AM voice, **F1D** = RTTY/FSK. The first letter gives the modulation type; the second the nature of the signal; the third the information type. [VERIFY] against ITU RR Appendix 1.

> [INFO] **Interactive candidate:** An emission designator decoder widget (`full-emission-designator-decoder.html`) — user enters a designator, widget decodes each character and states the amateur mode. This would directly reinforce 1I exam questions.

### Where You Will Actually Encounter These Designators

Knowing how to decode a designator is one thing; knowing where you will actually see one is another. Here are the most common practical touch-points:

**Ofcom Schedule 1 conditions.** The frequency allocation table attached to your licence specifies which modes are permitted or restricted on particular bands. Some entries reference emission designators directly — for example, restricting a shared allocation to specific signal types to protect the primary user.

**IARU Region 1 band plan.** The IARU band plan divides amateur HF and VHF bands into usage segments by designator. A segment described as "narrow-band modes: A1A, F1B, G1D, J2D" is telling you which coded signal types are expected there — and implicitly, that F3E (wideband FM voice) does not belong.

**Full licence exam questions.** The syllabus specifically tests decoding — expect questions such as "What does J3E mean?" or "Which designator describes FM voice?" A candidate who has internalised the three-character structure can answer without rote memorisation.

**Interference complaints and enforcement.** When Ofcom or a spectrum monitoring service documents interference, it records the emission type by designator. An enforcement notice stating "spurious emission F3E on [frequency]" is unambiguous in a way that "someone was using FM" is not.

**NoV applications.** Applications for a Notice of Variation — for example, to operate a special-event station with a unique callsign, or to request permissions outside standard licence conditions — may require you to list the emission types you intend to use.

> [INFO] **Worked scenario:** If Ofcom's conditions for 5 MHz state "permitted emissions: A1A, J3E, J2D" — you now know that authorises CW, SSB voice, and audio-subcarrier digital, but excludes FM voice (F3E) and direct-FM data (F1D). You can read the licence condition directly rather than waiting for someone to translate it for you.

### Why Bandwidth Terminology Matters in Practice

Three separate bandwidth definitions appear in the ITU Radio Regulations and in UK licence documentation. They often appear together in exam questions, which tempts candidates to treat them as similar-but-slightly-different things to memorise. They are not — each measures something genuinely different, and the practical difference matters when transmitters misbehave.

**Necessary bandwidth** is a design specification. It is the minimum bandwidth sufficient to carry the information at the required rate and quality. It is a theoretical floor, calculated from the signal type, not measured at the antenna. You encounter it in engineering documents and band-planning calculations.

**Occupied bandwidth** is a measurement. It is what your transmitter actually radiates — specifically, the frequency range containing 99% of the total radiated power. A spectrum analyser can measure it directly. When a regulator checks your signal, occupied bandwidth is what they observe.

**Assigned bandwidth** is a legal permission. It is the frequency range your licence authorises you to use, set wider than typical occupied bandwidth to accommodate practical transmitter imperfections.

> [INFO] A well-behaved transmitter: necessary bandwidth = 2.4 kHz (SSB voice, theoretical); occupied bandwidth = 2.8 kHz (what the antenna actually radiates, measured); assigned bandwidth = 3.0 kHz (what the licence authorises). Three different numbers describing the same signal.

The difference becomes critical when something goes wrong. If ALC (Automatic Level Control) is misadjusted on an SSB transmitter, intermodulation products splatter far outside the intended passband. The necessary bandwidth of the signal type has not changed. The assigned bandwidth in the licence has not changed. But the occupied bandwidth has grown — and if it exceeds the assigned bandwidth, the transmission is non-compliant regardless of how small the theoretical signal should be. Regulators measure occupied bandwidth. Your licence constrains you to assigned bandwidth. Keeping occupied bandwidth comfortably inside assigned bandwidth is both good practice and a legal obligation.

> [INFO] **Exam crib — bandwidth terms (ITU RR definitions):**
> - **Necessary bandwidth** — minimum bandwidth sufficient to carry the information at the required rate and quality. Theoretical; calculated from signal type.
> - **Occupied bandwidth** — range containing 99% of total mean power (0.5% excluded at each end). Measured; what a spectrum analyser shows.
> - **Assigned bandwidth** — the range the licence authorises. Widest of the three; set to accommodate real-world transmitter imperfections.
>

**CEPT Recommendation T/R 61-01** permits temporary non-resident operation up to 3 months for Full licence (HAREC) holders. Call sign format: **F/M0ABC** (host country code / your UK call sign). Carry paper copies of both your UK licence and the French amateur radio regulations.

<details>
<summary><strong>Q2: An Intermediate licensee asks if they can use T/R 61-01 to operate in Spain. What is the correct answer?</strong></summary>

**No.** CEPT T/R 61-01 applies to holders of a Full (personal) licence that carries a HAREC certificate. Intermediate licence holders do not hold a HAREC and cannot use T/R 61-01. They would need to arrange a temporary licence through bilateral agreement, if one exists with Spain.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Q3: What does the emission designator J3E describe?</strong></summary>

**J** = Single sideband, suppressed carrier. **3** = single channel of analogue information. **E** = telephony (voice). J3E is the standard emission type for SSB voice operation on amateur HF bands.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Q4: Your station runs 100 W PEP into an antenna with 3 dBi gain. When operating on 5 MHz, is your EIRP within the schedule limit?</strong></summary>

100 W PEP at 3 dBi = **200 W EIRP** (100 W × 2 = 200 W). Schedule note G I i sets a generic EIRP limit of 200 W at 5 MHz. This is exactly at the limit — compliant, but no margin. If the antenna gain were any higher or the power any greater the limit would be exceeded. [VERIFY] against current Ofcom schedule before operating on 5 MHz.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Q5: You operate from a temporary location. What identification obligations apply that differ from your home station?</strong></summary>

From a **temporary location** you must: (1) identify with your callsign every 15 minutes and on joining/leaving nets as normal; (2) give the temporary location every **30 minutes** (Schedule note G VIII); (3) have a telephone available at the number registered with Ofcom (Schedule note G IX). At your main station address you do not need to announce the location because it is your registered address.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Q6: A new Intermediate licensee's call sign begins with M8. What does this tell you?</strong></summary>

The **M8 and M9 series** were introduced for Intermediate licences from 2023. The M-series: M0/M1/M5 = Full; M3/M6/M7 = Foundation; M8/M9 = Intermediate (new series, replacing the 2E prefix series).

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Q7: Your 100 W FM transmitter on 2 m with a 6-element Yagi shows a required Ofcom Compliance Distance of 9.2 m. Your garden is 8 m wide and your neighbours' garden borders it. What must you do?</strong></summary>

The compliance distance (9.2 m) extends beyond your property boundary (8 m). You must either: (a) **reduce power or antenna gain** until the compliance distance fits within your property boundary, or (b) **restrict access** to the area within the compliance distance — e.g. ensure neighbours understand they should not be in that area during transmissions. Simply transmitting at 100 W would not be compliant. Use the Ofcom/RSGB EMF spreadsheet to find the combination of power, gain, and mode that brings the compliance distance within acceptable limits.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Q8: What is the difference between a Gateway and a Repeater in the context of unattended station operation?</strong></summary>

A **Repeater (6-15)** receives on one frequency and simultaneously re-transmits on another — extending range for mobile and portable stations; it stays within the amateur radio network. A **Gateway (6-14)** connects the amateur station to the internet or another non-amateur network — e.g. Echolink, D-STAR/YSF reflectors, IRLP. Both require compliance with the Notice of Co-ordination procedures if formal coordination is needed.

</details>
