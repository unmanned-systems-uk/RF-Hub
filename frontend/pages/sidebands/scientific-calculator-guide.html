Group 1: Ohm's Law and DC Power

These formulas apply at Foundation level and underpin everything else.

1.1 — Ohm's Law

V = I × R   ·   I = V / R   ·   R = V / I

Key sequences:

To find V: I × R = To find I: V ÷ R = To find R: V ÷ I =
  • 2 A through 47 Ω: 2 × 47 = → 94 V
  • Voltage 13.8 V, resistance 4.6 Ω: 13.8 ÷ 4.6 = → 3 A
  • 12 V supply, 0.5 A load: 12 ÷ 0.5 = → 24 Ω
⚠
Gotcha: Units must be consistent — volts, amps, ohms. Mixing mA and A gives a 1000× error. If you have 50 mA, enter 0.05 (amps).

1.2 — DC Power

P = V × I   =   I² × R   =   V² / R

Three equivalent forms — use whichever two quantities you know.

Know V and I: V × I = Know I and R: I x² × R = Know V and R: V x² ÷ R =
  • PA stage: V = 13.8 V, I = 5 A → 13.8 × 5 = → 69 W
  • Dummy load: I = 2 A, R = 50 Ω → 2 x² → 4 → × 50 = → 200 W
  • 5 W into 50 Ω: 5 × 50 = → 250 → ²√x → 15.8 V (rearrangement: V = √(P × R))
  • 100 W into 75 Ω feeder: 100 × 75 = → 7500 → ²√x → 86.6 V RMS across the feeder
⚠
Gotcha: These are DC or RMS power formulas. Peak voltage is VRMS × √2. For an RF carrier at 100 W into 50 Ω: VRMS = √(100×50) = 70.7 V RMS; Vpeak = 70.7 × 1.414 = 100 V peak.

1.3 — Resistors in Parallel

R_total = (R₁ × R₂) / (R₁ + R₂)    [two resistors] 1/R_total = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + 1/R₃ + ...    [three or more]

Key sequence (two resistors):

( R₁ × R₂ ) ÷ ( R₁ + R₂ ) = Or reciprocal method (any number of resistors): 1. Type R₁ → ¹⁄ₓ → M+ (store 1/R₁) 2. Type R₂ → ¹⁄ₓ → M+ (add 1/R₂ to memory) 3. MR → ¹⁄ₓ → result is R_total
  • 100 Ω ∥ 100 Ω: ( 100 × 100 ) ÷ ( 100 + 100 ) = → 50 Ω
  • 50 Ω ∥ 75 Ω: ( 50 × 75 ) ÷ ( 50 + 75 ) = → 3750 ÷ 125 → 30 Ω
  • Three 150 Ω resistors: 150 ¹⁄ₓ M+; 150 ¹⁄ₓ M+; 150 ¹⁄ₓ M+; MR ¹⁄ₓ → 50 Ω
⚠
Gotcha: Parallel resistance is always smaller than the smallest individual resistor. If your answer is bigger than any of the inputs, you've calculated series instead of parallel.

Group 2: Decibels

2.1 — Power Ratio to dB

dB = 10 × log(P_out / P_in)
1. Type P_out ÷ P_in = → ratio on screen 2. log → log₁₀ of ratio 3. × 10 = → result in dB
  • 5 W in, 80 W out: 80 ÷ 5 = → 16 → log → 1.204 → × 10 = → 12.04 dB
  • 400 W in, 100 W out (loss): 100 ÷ 400 = → 0.25 → log → −0.602 → × 10 = → −6.02 dB
  • 2 W in, 100 W out: 100 ÷ 2 = → 50 → log → 1.699 → × 10 = → 16.99 dB
  • Feeder loss: 50 W TX, 32 W at antenna: 32 ÷ 50 = → 0.64 → log → −0.194 → × 10 = → −1.94 dB

2.2 — dB Back to Power

P_out = P_in × 10^(dB / 10)
1. Type the dB value (negative for loss) 2. ÷ 10 = → dB/10 3. 10ˣ → power ratio 4. × P_in = → output power in watts
  • 13 dB gain, 2 W in: 13 ÷ 10 = → 1.3 → 10ˣ → 19.95 → × 2 = → 39.9 W ≈ 40 W
  • −3 dB loss, 100 W in: 3 +/− ÷ 10 = → −0.3 → 10ˣ → 0.5 → × 100 = → 50 W
  • Amp claims +17 dB, input 0.5 W: 17 ÷ 10 = → 1.7 → 10ˣ → 50.12 → × 0.5 = → 25.06 W
  • 3 dB coax loss each way, 25 W output: path loss = 6 dB → 6 +/− ÷ 10 = → −0.6 → 10ˣ → 0.25 → × 25 = → 6.25 W reaching antenna
⚠
Gotcha: Use 10ˣ here, not eˣ (2ⁿᵈ ln). The 10ˣ button is the inverse of log (base 10). Using eˣ is the inverse of ln and gives a completely different result.

2.3 — Voltage Gain in dB

dB = 20 × log(V_out / V_in)
1. Type V_out ÷ V_in = 2. log 3. × 20 = → result in dB
  • 10 mV in, 500 mV out: 500 ÷ 10 = → 50 → log → 1.699 → × 20 = → 33.98 dB
  • 1 V in, 1 V out (unity gain): 1 ÷ 1 = → 1 → log → 0 → × 20 = → 0 dB ✓
  • Attenuator: 2 V in, 0.5 V out: 0.5 ÷ 2 = → 0.25 → log → −0.602 → × 20 = → −12.04 dB
⚠
Gotcha: Voltage uses ×20; power uses ×10. Mixing them gives an answer exactly 2× wrong in dB. Rule: 6 dB = ×2 in voltage, but 6 dB = ×4 in power (because P ∝ V², so doubling V quadruples P).

2.4 — Absolute Power: dBW and dBm

Sections 2.1–2.3 express relative change. dBW and dBm are absolute power levels — they tell you the actual power in watts using a fixed reference.

dBW = 10 × log₁₀(P / 1 W) = 10 × log₁₀(P) dBm = 10 × log₁₀(P / 1 mW) = 10 × log₁₀(P / 0.001) dBm = dBW + 30

dBW (reference: 1 watt):

Type P (watts) → log → × 10 = → dBW

dBm (reference: 1 milliwatt):

Method A (from dBW): + 30 = → dBm Method B (direct): P (watts) ÷ 0.001 = → log → × 10 =

Converting dBm back to watts:

dBm value − 30 = (gives dBW) → ÷ 10 = → 10ˣ → power in watts

Group 3: Reactance and Impedance

ℹ
Unit reminder before every calculation: f in Hz (not MHz), C in farads (not pF), L in henries (not μH).

3.1 — Capacitive Reactance

X_C = 1 / (2π × f × C)

Higher frequency or larger capacitance → lower X_C → capacitor passes signal more easily.

1. 2 × π × f (Hz) × C (farads) = → denominator 2. ¹⁄ₓ → X_C in ohms
  • f = 7.1 MHz, C = 220 pF: 2 × π × 7100000 × 0.00000000022 = → 0.009815 → ¹⁄ₓ → 101.9 Ω
  • f = 3.5 MHz, C = 100 pF: 2 × π × 3500000 × 0.0000000001 = → 0.002199 → ¹⁄ₓ → 454.7 Ω
  • f = 144 MHz, C = 10 pF: 2 × π × 144000000 × 0.00000000001 = → 0.009047 → ¹⁄ₓ → 110.5 Ω
  • Bypass cap at 14 MHz, need X_C < 5 Ω: C = 1/(2π × 14,000,000 × 5) → denominator: 2 × π × 14000000 × 5 = → 439,822,971 → ¹⁄ₓ → 2.27 nF minimum
⚠
Gotcha: 220 pF = 220 × 10⁻¹² = 0.00000000022 F. Forgetting the conversion produces a reactance 10¹² times wrong. Always write out the decimal first: count the zeros for picofarads (12 decimal places).

3.2 — Inductive Reactance

X_L = 2π × f × L

Higher frequency or larger inductance → higher X_L → inductor opposes signal more strongly.

2 × π × f (Hz) × L (henries) = → X_L in ohms
  • f = 14.2 MHz, L = 2.5 μH (0.0000025 H): 2 × π × 14200000 × 0.0000025 = → 223.1 Ω
  • RFC: L = 100 μH (0.0001 H), f = 7.1 MHz: 2 × π × 7100000 × 0.0001 = → 4461 Ω (very high — good choke)
  • f = 144 MHz, L = 10 nH (0.00000001 H): 2 × π × 144000000 × 0.00000001 = → 9.047 Ω
  • Verify resonance: L = 1 μH, C = 100 pF, claimed f₀ = 15.92 MHz → X_L = X_C = 100.0 Ω ✓
⚠
Gotcha: X_L and X_C both change with frequency, but in opposite directions. At resonance they are equal and opposite — they cancel, leaving only R in the circuit. If X_L = X_C, you're at f₀.

3.3 — Series Impedance Magnitude

Z = √(R² + X²)

X is the net reactance: if both X_L and X_C are present, use X = |X_L − X_C|.

1. Type R → x² → + 2. Type X (net reactance) → x² → = 3. ²√x → Z in ohms
  • R = 50 Ω, X_L = 120 Ω: 50 x² → 2500 → + 120 x² → 14400 → = → 16900 → ²√x → 130 Ω
  • R = 75 Ω, X_C = 100 Ω: 75 x² → 5625 → + 100 x² → 10000 → = → 15625 → ²√x → 125 Ω
  • Antenna feedpoint: R_rad = 35 Ω, X = +25 Ω: 35 x² → 1225 → + 25 x² → 625 → = → 1850 → ²√x → 43.0 Ω
  • Verify 50 Ω match: R = 48 Ω, X = 14 Ω: 48 x² → 2304 → + 14 x² → 196 → = → 2500 → ²√x → 50.0 Ω ✓
⚠
Gotcha: This formula gives only the magnitude of impedance. Phase angle requires arctan(X/R). Always use the net reactance (X_L − X_C if both present), not the sum.

Group 4: Resonance

4.1 — Resonant Frequency

f₀ = 1 / (2π × √(L × C))

L in henries, C in farads, result in Hz. Divide by 10⁶ to get MHz.

1. Type L (H) × C (F) = → LC product (very small — scientific notation normal) 2. ²√x → √(LC) 3. × 2 × π = → 2π√(LC) 4. ¹⁄ₓ → f₀ in Hz 5. ÷ 1000000 = → f₀ in MHz
  • L = 1 μH, C = 100 pF: 0.000001 × 0.0000000001 = → 1e-16 → ²√x → 1e-8 → × 2 × π = → 6.283e-8 → ¹⁄ₓ → 15.92 MHz
  • Tank circuit: L = 10 μH, C = 68 pF: 0.00001 × 0.000000000068 = → 6.8e-16 → ²√x → 2.608e-8 → × 2 × π = → 1.639e-7 → ¹⁄ₓ → 6.1 MHz
  • Crystal filter at 10.7 MHz — what C with L = 1 μH? C = 1/(4π²f²L): 4 × π x² × 10700000 x² × 0.000001 = → 4,523,893,944 → ¹⁄ₓ → 221 pF
⚠
Gotcha: The intermediate result (L × C) will display in scientific notation like 1e-16. This is normal and correct. Continue pressing keys; the final answer resolves to a sensible Hz value. If you see 0 after pressing ²√x, you may have mis-entered a value.

4.2 — Q Factor

Q = X_L / R    (at resonance; dimensionless)

Q describes selectivity: high Q = narrow bandwidth. Calculate X_L at f₀ first.

1. Calculate X_L at f₀ (use Formula 3.2) 2. Type X_L ÷ R = → Q
  • f₀ = 15.92 MHz, L = 1 μH → X_L = 100 Ω. R = 2 Ω: 100 ÷ 2 = → Q = 50
  • AM IF filter: L = 200 μH, f = 455 kHz, R = 5.75 Ω: X_L: 2 × π × 455000 × 0.0002 = → 572.1 → ÷ 5.75 = → Q ≈ 99.5
  • Find R_loss: L = 10 μH, f = 7 MHz, Q = 80. X_L = 439.8 Ω. R = X_L/Q: 439.8 ÷ 80 = → R_loss = 5.5 Ω
⚠
Gotcha: Q is frequency-dependent because X_L varies with frequency. Always calculate X_L at the resonant frequency f₀, not at some other test frequency.

4.3 — 3 dB Bandwidth

BW = f₀ / Q

The frequency span between the points where response has fallen 3 dB from peak.

Type f₀ (Hz) ÷ Q = → BW in Hz ÷ 1000 = → BW in kHz
  • f₀ = 15.92 MHz, Q = 50: 15920000 ÷ 50 = → 318.4 kHz
  • AM IF filter: f₀ = 455 kHz, Q = 99.5: 455000 ÷ 99.5 = → 4573 Hz ≈ 4.6 kHz
  • SSB filter: f₀ = 9 MHz, BW = 2.4 kHz → Q needed: 9000000 ÷ 2400 = → Q = 3750 (crystal or mechanical filter required)
  • 2 m front end: f₀ = 144 MHz, Q = 20: 144000000 ÷ 20 = → 7.2 MHz bandwidth (very broad — expected for RF front end)
⚠
Gotcha: The result is in Hz if you enter f₀ in Hz. Always enter f₀ in Hz, always get BW in Hz, then convert to kHz by ÷ 1000.

Group 5: SWR and Reflection

5.1 — VSWR from Reflection Coefficient

VSWR = (1 + |Γ|) / (1 − |Γ|)

|Γ| ranges from 0 (perfect match) to 1 (total reflection).

( 1 + |Γ| ) ÷ ( 1 − |Γ| ) = → VSWR
  • |Γ| = 0.333: ( 1 + 0.333 ) ÷ ( 1 − 0.333 ) = → VSWR = 2.0:1
  • |Γ| = 0.5: ( 1 + 0.5 ) ÷ ( 1 − 0.5 ) = → VSWR = 3.0:1
  • |Γ| = 0.1: ( 1 + 0.1 ) ÷ ( 1 − 0.1 ) = → VSWR = 1.22:1 (excellent)
  • Open circuit (|Γ| = 1): denominator = 0 → VSWR = ∞
⚠
Gotcha: |Γ| must be between 0 and 1. Entering |Γ| ≥ 1 gives division by zero or a negative — physically impossible. A perfect match is |Γ| = 0, VSWR = 1:1.

5.2 — Reflection Coefficient from VSWR

|Γ| = (VSWR − 1) / (VSWR + 1)
( VSWR − 1 ) ÷ ( VSWR + 1 ) = → |Γ|
  • VSWR = 2.5:1: ( 2.5 − 1 ) ÷ ( 2.5 + 1 ) = → |Γ| = 0.4286
  • VSWR = 3:1: ( 3 − 1 ) ÷ ( 3 + 1 ) = → |Γ| = 0.5
  • VSWR = 1.5:1: ( 1.5 − 1 ) ÷ ( 1.5 + 1 ) = → |Γ| = 0.2
  • VSWR = 10:1: ( 10 − 1 ) ÷ ( 10 + 1 ) = → |Γ| = 0.818
⚠
Gotcha: Enter VSWR as a plain number. If your meter shows "2.5:1", enter just 2.5. The ":1" part is implied.

5.3 — Return Loss

RL = 20 × log(1 / |Γ|)    (positive dB — higher is better)
1. 1 ÷ |Γ| = 2. log 3. × 20 = → return loss in dB (positive)
  • |Γ| = 0.4286 (VSWR 2.5:1): 1 ÷ 0.4286 = → 2.333 → log → 0.368 → × 20 = → 7.36 dB
  • |Γ| = 0.333 (VSWR 2:1): 1 ÷ 0.333 = → 3.003 → log → 0.4775 → × 20 = → 9.55 dB
  • |Γ| = 0.1 (VSWR 1.22:1): 1 ÷ 0.1 = → 10 → log → 1 → × 20 = → 20 dB (excellent)
  • Target RL > 30 dB → |Γ| < ?: 30 ÷ 20 = → 1.5 → 10ˣ → 31.62 → ¹⁄ₓ → |Γ| < 0.0316
⚠
Gotcha: Return loss must be positive. If you get a negative answer, you forgot to use 1/|Γ|. RL > 20 dB is a good match; > 30 dB is excellent.

5.4 — Power Reflected

P_reflected = |Γ|² × P_incident
|Γ| x² × P_incident = → P_reflected (watts) Then: P_incident − P_reflected = → power delivered to load
  • VSWR 2.5:1 (|Γ| = 0.4286), 100 W: 0.4286 x² → 0.1837 → × 100 = → 18.37 W reflected; 81.63 W to antenna
  • VSWR 3:1 (|Γ| = 0.5), 50 W: 0.5 x² → 0.25 → × 50 = → 12.5 W reflected (25%)
  • VSWR 1.5:1 (|Γ| = 0.2), 100 W: 0.2 x² → 0.04 → × 100 = → 4 W reflected (only 4%)
⚠
Gotcha: Many people overestimate power loss from modest VSWR. VSWR 2:1 only reflects 11% of power. Coax heating from mismatch is often more significant than the power not reaching the antenna.

Group 6: Propagation and Wavelength

6.1 — Free-Space Wavelength

λ (m) = 300 / f_MHz

f in MHz, result in metres. Antenna element lengths derive directly from this.

300 ÷ f (MHz) = → full wavelength (m) ÷ 2 = → half-wavelength ÷ 2 = again → quarter-wavelength
  • 40 m band, 7.1 MHz: 300 ÷ 7.1 = → 42.25 m; ÷ 2 = 21.1 m (half-wave dipole total); ÷ 2 = 10.6 m per element
  • 20 m band, 14.225 MHz: 300 ÷ 14.225 = → 21.09 m; half-wave = 10.5 m total
  • 2 m band, 145 MHz: 300 ÷ 145 = → 2.069 m; quarter-wave: ÷ 4 = → 51.7 cm
  • 70 cm band, 433 MHz: 300 ÷ 433 = → 0.693 m; half-wave = 34.6 cm; quarter-wave = 17.3 cm

In practice, a half-wave wire dipole is cut about 5% shorter than λ/2 due to end effects. Field calculation shortcut: element length ≈ 143 / f_MHz metres.

⚠
Gotcha: f must be in MHz. If you enter f in Hz, you get wavelength in megametres, not metres. Sanity check: at 7 MHz the answer should be around 43 m, not 43,000 m.

6.2 — Maximum Usable Frequency (MUF)

MUF = f_c × √(1 + (d / 2h)²)

f_c is the vertical-incidence critical frequency; d is path distance; h is layer height. Keep d and h in the same units.

1. d ÷ 2 ÷ h = → (d/2h) 2. x² → (d/2h)² 3. + 1 = → 1 + (d/2h)² 4. ²√x → obliquity factor 5. × f_c = → MUF OWF: × 0.85 = → Optimum Working Frequency
  • §12 example: f_c = 9 MHz, d = 2000 km, h = 300 km: 2000 ÷ 2 ÷ 300 = → 3.333 → x² → 11.11 → + 1 = → 12.11 → ²√x → 3.479 → × 9 = → 31.3 MHz; OWF: × 0.85 = 26.6 MHz
  • §12 Q19: f_c = 8 MHz, d = 3000 km, h = 300 km: 3000 ÷ 2 ÷ 300 = → 5.0 → x² → 25 → + 1 = → 26 → ²√x → 5.099 → × 8 = → 40.8 MHz
  • §12 Q25: foF2 = 6 MHz, d = 1800 km, h = 300 km: 1800 ÷ 2 ÷ 300 = → 3.0 → x² → 9 → + 1 = → 10 → ²√x → 3.162 → × 6 = → 18.97 MHz ≈ 19 MHz MUF
⚠
Gotcha: d and h must be in identical units. Mixing km and metres produces an answer off by 1000×. No DEG/RAD concern here — no trig functions are called directly in this formula.

6.3 — Cable Wavelength and Velocity Factor

λ_cable = (300 / f_MHz) × VF

VF (velocity factor) is always < 1. Quarter-wave stub = λ_cable / 4.

300 ÷ f (MHz) × VF = → full wavelength in cable (m) ÷ 4 = → quarter-wave length (m) × 100 = → in centimetres
  • §11 example: λ/4 stub, f = 144 MHz, RG-58 (VF = 0.66): 300 ÷ 144 × 0.66 = → 1.375 m → ÷ 4 = → 34.4 cm
  • Coax trap at 7 MHz, foam coax (VF = 0.82): 300 ÷ 7 × 0.82 = → 35.14 m → ÷ 2 = → 17.57 m half-wave trap
  • 28 MHz λ/4 stub, air-spaced (VF ≈ 0.95): 300 ÷ 28 × 0.95 = → 10.18 m → ÷ 4 = → 2.54 m

Gotcha: VF is always less than 1.0. If your result is longer than the free-space wavelength, you accidentally used VF > 1. Air-spaced line (VF ≈ 0.97) is closest to free space; solid PE fills slow the wave to VF ≈ 0.66.

Group 7: Impedance Matching and Transformers

7.1 — Quarter-Wave Impedance Transformer

Z₀ = √(Z_source × Z_load)

The characteristic impedance of the λ/4 matching section needed to match two impedances.

Z_source × Z_load = → then ²√x → Z₀ in ohms
  • §11 example: match 25 Ω antenna to 50 Ω coax: 25 × 50 = → 1250 → ²√x → 35.4 Ω
  • Match 75 Ω to 300 Ω: 75 × 300 = → 22500 → ²√x → 150 Ω matching section required
  • Match 50 Ω to 50 Ω (unity): 50 × 50 = → 2500 → ²√x → 50 Ω ✓
⚠
Gotcha: The λ/4 transformer only works at one frequency (and its odd harmonics). Use Formula 6.3 to calculate the physical length in your cable type.

7.2 — Transformer Turns Ratio and Voltage

V₁ / V₂ = N₁ / N₂     I₁ / I₂ = N₂ / N₁
Find V₂: V₁ × N₂ ÷ N₁ = Find N₂: V₂ × N₁ ÷ V₁ = Find step ratio: V₁ ÷ V₂ = → ratio (N₁:N₂)
  • Mains transformer: 240 V, N₁ = 480, N₂ = 60: 240 × 60 ÷ 480 = → 30 V output
  • 13.8 V from 240 V mains: 240 ÷ 13.8 = → 17.4:1 step-down
  • Step-up: 12 V, N₁ = 10, N₂ = 50: 12 × 50 ÷ 10 = → 60 V
  • Current: V₁ = 240 V, V₂ = 30 V, I₁ = 1 A → I₂ = 1 × 8 = 8 A (240×1 = 30×8 = 240 VA ✓)
⚠
Gotcha: Transformers conserve power (V₁ × I₁ ≈ V₂ × I₂). If V steps up, I steps down by the same ratio. A step-up voltage transformer is a step-down current transformer.

7.3 — Impedance Transformation

Z₁ / Z₂ = (N₁ / N₂)²     N₁ / N₂ = √(Z₁ / Z₂)
Find turns ratio from impedance ratio: Z₁ ÷ Z₂ = → ²√x → N₁/N₂ turns ratio Find Z₂: Z₁ × N₂ x² ÷ N₁ x² =
  • 4:1 balun — what turns ratio? 4 ²√x → 2:1 turns ratio
  • Match 50 Ω to 200 Ω: 200 ÷ 50 = → 4 → ²√x → 2:1 turns ratio needed
  • §11 quiz: 4:1 balun on 150 Ω balanced antenna → unbalanced: 150 ÷ 4 = → 37.5 Ω; VSWR vs 50 Ω: ( 50 − 37.5 ) ÷ ( 50 + 37.5 ) = → 0.143 → VSWR formula → 1.33:1 (acceptable)
  • 9:1 unun for EFHW (2450 Ω to 50 Ω): 9 ²√x → 3:1 turns ratio
⚠
Gotcha: Impedance transforms as the square of turns ratio — not linearly. A 2:1 turns ratio gives 4:1 impedance change. A 3:1 turns ratio gives 9:1 impedance change.

Group 8: Time Constants and Exponential Decay

8.1 — RC Time Constant

τ = R × C    (τ in seconds; R in Ω, C in farads)
R (Ω) × C (F) = → τ in seconds
  • R = 100 kΩ (100,000 Ω), C = 47 μF (0.000047 F): 100000 × 0.000047 = → τ = 4.7 s
  • Timing circuit: R = 47 kΩ, C = 10 μF (0.00001 F): 47000 × 0.00001 = → τ = 0.47 s
  • Fast discharge: R = 10 Ω, C = 1000 μF (0.001 F): 10 × 0.001 = → τ = 10 ms
  • Wanted τ = 1 s, C = 100 μF → R: 1 ÷ 0.0001 = → R = 10 kΩ
⚠
Gotcha: C must be in farads. Entering 47 instead of 0.000047 (forgetting the μ) gives τ = 4,700,000 s — over 54 days — rather than 4.7 s.

8.2 — RL Time Constant

τ = L / R    (note: division — opposite to RC)
L (H) ÷ R (Ω) = → τ in seconds
  • L = 50 mH (0.05 H), R = 25 Ω: 0.05 ÷ 25 = → τ = 2 ms
  • Relay coil: L = 100 mH (0.1 H), R = 200 Ω: 0.1 ÷ 200 = → τ = 0.5 ms
  • RF choke: L = 1 mH (0.001 H), R = 0.5 Ω: 0.001 ÷ 0.5 = → τ = 2 ms
⚠
Gotcha: The formula is L/R for inductors, R×C for capacitors. Memory aid: for an inductor, higher R drains stored energy faster (shorter τ); for a capacitor, higher R slows the charging path (longer τ).

8.3 — Exponential Charge and Discharge

Discharging: V(t) = V₀ × e^(−t/τ) Charging: V(t) = V_final × (1 − e^(−t/τ))

Key sequence — discharging (cleanest method via reciprocal):

1. Press e → xʸ → type t/τ → = → e^(t/τ) 2. ¹⁄ₓ → e^(−t/τ) 3. × V₀ = → voltage remaining

Key sequence — charging:

1. e → xʸ → t/τ → = → e^(t/τ) 2. ¹⁄ₓ → e^(−t/τ) 3. +/− → −e^(−t/τ) 4. + 1 = → (1 − e^(−t/τ)) 5. × V_final = → voltage charged to
  • Discharge: V₀ = 12 V, after 3τ: e xʸ 3 = → 20.086 → ¹⁄ₓ → 0.04979 → × 12 = → 0.60 V
  • Discharge: 200 V cap, after 2τ: e xʸ 2 = → 7.389 → ¹⁄ₓ → 0.1353 → × 200 = → 27.07 V remaining
  • Charge: V_final = 12 V, after 2τ: → e^(2) → 7.389 → ¹⁄ₓ → 0.1353 → +/− → + 1 = → 0.8647 → × 12 = → 10.38 V
  • Charge: V_final = 100 V, after 1τ: → (1 − e⁻¹) × 100 = 63.21 V (definition of one time constant)

Group 9: ADC, SDR, and Digital Signal Processing

These formulas apply at Full licence level (RSGB Ch. 10).

9.1 — ADC Dynamic Range

DR ≈ 6 × n dB    (n = bit depth) Precise: DR = 6.02n + 1.76 dB
Approximate: n × 6 = → DR in dB Precise: n × 6.02 + 1.76 =
  • 8-bit (RTL-SDR dongle): 8 × 6 = → 48 dB
  • 12-bit ADC: 12 × 6 = → 72 dB
  • 14-bit HF SDR: 14 × 6 = → 84 dB
  • 16-bit ADC: 16 × 6 = → 96 dB
  • Need 100 dB DR — how many bits? 100 ÷ 6 = → 16.67 → 17 bits minimum
⚠
Gotcha: Each additional bit adds ~6 dB. Going from 8-bit to 16-bit is 8 bits × 6 dB = 48 dB improvement — why professional SDRs use 14- or 16-bit ADCs despite the higher cost.

9.2 — Nyquist Bandwidth

BW_max = f_sample / 2
f_sample (Hz or Msps) ÷ 2 = → maximum usable bandwidth
  • RTL-SDR: 2.4 Msps → 2.4 ÷ 2 = → 1.2 MHz usable bandwidth
  • HackRF One: 20 Msps → 20 ÷ 2 = → 10 MHz
  • Direct-sampling HF: 122.88 Msps → 122.88 ÷ 2 = → 61.44 MHz — entire HF spectrum at once
  • SSB voice (2.4 kHz signal): minimum sample rate: 2.4 × 2 = → 4.8 kHz minimum (8 kHz standard in practice)
⚠
Gotcha: This is the theoretical Nyquist limit. Real SDR software uses 80–90% of theoretical bandwidth to avoid edge artefacts. A 2.4 Msps dongle gives usable bandwidth of about 1–1.8 MHz depending on software.

9.3 — FFT Frequency Resolution (Bin Width)

bin width = f_sample / N_FFT

N_FFT must be a power of 2. Result is the frequency spacing between displayed spectrum bins.

f_sample (Hz) ÷ N_FFT = → bin width in Hz
  • RTL-SDR, FFT = 1024: 2400000 ÷ 1024 = → 2343.75 Hz ≈ 2.3 kHz per bin (coarse)
  • Same SDR, FFT = 65536: 2400000 ÷ 65536 = → 36.6 Hz per bin (fine detail)
  • HackRF: 10,000,000 Hz, FFT = 4096: 10000000 ÷ 4096 = → 2441 Hz per bin
  • Resolve signals 500 Hz apart, 1 MHz sample rate → minimum N_FFT: 1000000 ÷ 500 = → 2000 → round up to 2048
⚠
Gotcha: Larger FFT = finer frequency resolution but slower update rate and more CPU load. A 65k-point FFT takes over 27× longer to compute than a 2k FFT. Most SDR software lets you adjust to trade resolution against waterfall speed.
