NATO Phonetic Alphabet

Say It So It's Heard. The standard alphabet that turns letters into words that can't be misheard — over static, fading, and interference.

RSGB 8A1 RSGB 8B1 Foundation Intermediate Full
01

Why Phonetics?

The NATO phonetic alphabet replaces letters with words that can't be misheard — even over a noisy radio link. When you say "Mike Seven Alpha Bravo Charlie" instead of "M-7-A-B-C", the other station knows exactly what you said, regardless of static, fading, or interference.

Every amateur radio operator uses it. Learn it. It's one of the first things you'll need on air.

04

When to Use It

Always Use Phonetics For:

  • Your callsign — every time you give it: "Mike Seven Alpha Bravo Charlie"
  • The other station's callsign — on first contact or if you're unsure they heard you
  • Grid squares and locators — "India Oscar Nine One Alpha Sierra"
  • Unusual words — names, place names, anything that might be misheard

You Don't Need Phonetics For:

  • Common Q-codes — "QTH" is universally understood without spelling out
  • Signal reports — "Five nine" not "Fife Niner" (though both work)
  • Numbers in context — frequencies are spoken normally: "One four four point three zero zero"

06

Callsign Suffixes

When you operate away from your main station address, you add a suffix to your callsign to indicate your situation. These are legal requirements under UK licence conditions — not optional.

SuffixMeaningExampleWhen to Use
/M Mobile M7ABC/M Operating from a car, on foot, or on a vessel in inland waters
/P Portable M7ABC/P Temporary location with no fixed postal address (a field, hilltop, park)
/A Alternative address M7ABC/A Operating from a different postal address to your licence address (friend's house, hotel, holiday home)
/MM Maritime Mobile M0PPP/MM Operating from a vessel at sea
/AM Aeronautical Mobile M0PPP/AM Operating from an aircraft (max 500 mW EIRP, primary bands only)
🗣️
How to say it: "Mike Seven Alpha Bravo Charlie stroke Mobile" — the "/" is spoken as "stroke" in the UK, or "slash" or "portable" informally. On CW, the "/" is sent as −··−· (dah-dit-dit-dah-dit).

📋
2024 update: Ofcom now allows flexible suffixes in addition to the standard ones. You can append descriptive suffixes like "/Garden" or "/Hilltop" — but the standard suffixes (/M, /P, /A, /MM, /AM) still carry their legal meanings and should be used correctly.

07

Practice Tips

  1. Start with your own callsign. Say it out loud using phonetics until it's automatic.
  2. Read car number plates. Great daily practice — spell each one phonetically in your head.
  3. Listen to HF. Tune to 14.300 MHz (20m SSB) or 7.100 MHz (40m SSB) and listen to how experienced operators use phonetics naturally.
  4. Speed comes with use. Don't worry about being slow at first — accuracy matters more than speed.

Common Mistakes

MistakeProblemCorrect
"A for Apple"Non-standard — not everyone knows your versionUse "Alpha"
"N for Nancy"Old RAF phonetic — confusing for international contactsUse "November"
"O for Orange"AmbiguousUse "Oscar"
Skipping phonetics on a weak signalStation can't copy your callsignAlways use phonetics when conditions are poor
Speaking too fastDefeats the purposeSpeak clearly and at a moderate pace
⚠️
Stick to NATO. You'll occasionally hear operators use non-standard words ("London", "America", "Nancy"). This works fine domestically but causes confusion in international contacts. The NATO alphabet is understood worldwide — there's no good reason to use anything else.
