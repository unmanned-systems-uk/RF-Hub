QSO Scripts — What to Say and When to Say It

Step-by-step scripts for FM simplex, repeaters, HF SSB, CW, digital voice, and nets

F I FL RSGB 8A1 8B1 8C1
Series: Sideband — Topic Snippets for RF-Hub Cross-refs: Q-Codes · Band Plan · Operating Procedures

Making your first contact on the radio can be nerve-wracking. You know the theory, you've passed the exam, but what do you actually say? These scripts give you a step-by-step guide for the most common types of contact. Follow them until the procedure feels natural — then make them your own.

Before You Start — The Golden Rules

  1. 1Listen first. Before transmitting on any frequency, listen for at least 30 seconds. On a calling frequency, ask "Is this frequency in use?" before calling CQ.
  2. 2Keep it legal. Always give your callsign at the start and end of a contact, and at least every 15 minutes during a long QSO.
  3. 3Be brief on calling frequencies. Make contact, exchange callsigns, then move to a clear frequency. Don't have your whole conversation on the calling frequency.
  4. 4Speak clearly and steadily. No need to shout or rush. Use the NATO phonetic alphabet for your callsign: "Mike Seven Alpha Bravo Charlie" not "M-7-A-B-C."
  5. 5Don't panic. Everyone was new once. If you make a mistake, correct yourself and carry on. The amateur radio community is welcoming to new operators.

1

VHF/UHF FM Simplex

145.500 MHz (2m) or 433.500 MHz (70cm) FM calling frequencies

How it works: You call on the calling frequency, make contact, then both stations move to a clear channel to have your conversation. Never hold a long QSO on the calling frequency.

Step 1 — Call CQ on the calling frequency

Tune to 145.500 MHz (2m) or 433.500 MHz (70cm). Listen for 30 seconds to check it's clear.
Say: "CQ CQ CQ, this is YOUR CALLSIGN, YOUR CALLSIGN, YOUR CALLSIGN. Calling CQ and standing by."

Repeat two or three times with a short pause between each call. If no one responds, wait a minute and try again.

Step 2 — Someone responds

A station comes back to your call.
They say: "YOUR CALLSIGN, this is THEIR CALLSIGN. Good evening, receiving you well."

Step 3 — Acknowledge and find a clear frequency

Say: "THEIR CALLSIGN, this is YOUR CALLSIGN. Good evening, thanks for coming back to me. Please stand by while I find a clear frequency."

Tune up or down from the calling frequency to find an unused simplex channel. Listen for a few seconds to confirm it's clear.

Step 4 — Check the new frequency is clear

On the new frequency, say: "This is YOUR CALLSIGN — is this frequency in use?"

Wait 10 seconds. If no one responds, it's clear. If someone says "Yes" or "QRL", try another channel.

Step 5 — Call the other station to the new frequency

Go back to 145.500 MHz and say: "THEIR CALLSIGN, this is YOUR CALLSIGN. Please QSY to [new frequency]."

Both stations change to the new frequency.

Step 6 — Start the conversation

On the new frequency, say: "THEIR CALLSIGN, this is YOUR CALLSIGN. Are you receiving? Over."
When they confirm, say: "THEIR CALLSIGN, this is YOUR CALLSIGN. My name is [your name] and my QTH is [your location]. Your signal report is [RS report, e.g., 5 and 9]. How do you copy? THEIR CALLSIGN, this is YOUR CALLSIGN. Over."

Step 7 — Close the contact

When you're ready to finish, say: "THEIR CALLSIGN, this is YOUR CALLSIGN. Thanks for the contact today. I hope to speak with you again soon. 73 and best wishes. THEIR CALLSIGN, this is YOUR CALLSIGN. Out."
"Over" vs "Out"

"Over" means "I've finished speaking, go ahead" — you expect a reply. "Out" means "I've finished, conversation is ending" — no reply expected. Never say "over and out" — it contradicts itself.

Where to QSY — The 2m FM Channel Grid

When someone responds to your CQ on 145.500 and you say "please QSY to 145.450" — that works because 145.450 is a real channel. But if you said "let's go to 145.465" you'd be between channels, and the other station's radio might not tune there cleanly.

The 2m FM simplex range uses a 12.5 kHz channel grid defined by the RSGB/IARU Region 1 band plan:

Range145.200 – 145.5935 MHz
Spacing12.5 kHz
ChannelsV16 – V47
FormulaVn = 145.000 + (n × 0.0125) MHz

So V40 = 145.000 + (40 × 0.0125) = 145.500 MHz — the calling frequency.

Channels to Keep Clear

These frequencies have designated uses — don't QSY to them for a general chat:

Free Channels — Where to Go

Everything else is fair game. The most commonly used QSY destinations:

Below calling Popular — most operators move down:
145.425 · 145.4375 · 145.450 · 145.4625 · 145.475
Above calling 145.5125 · 145.5375 · 145.5625
Wide open 145.275 · 145.300 · 145.325 and 145.350 – 145.4875

The Even/Odd Channel Trick

The even V-channels match the old 25 kHz "S-channel" grid — S20 = 145.500, S18 = 145.450, S16 = 145.400. Older rigs and experienced operators default to these, so they're busier.

If the band is crowded, try the odd 12.5 kHz channels: 145.4625 (V37), 145.4875 (V39), 145.5375 (V43). They're often empty because many operators still think in 25 kHz steps.

Danger Zones — Never Transmit Here
Above 145.5935 MHz Repeater outputs start at RV48 (145.600) — you'd be transmitting on top of repeater output signals
145.800 – 146.000 MHz Satellite-exclusive — never use for simplex. You could interfere with ISS or amateur satellite downlinks

The QSY Procedure

  1. Someone responds to your CQ on 145.500
  2. Pick a channel from the free list (e.g., 145.450 = V36)
  3. Tune to it and listen for 10 seconds
  4. Ask: "This is YOUR CALLSIGN — is this frequency in use?"
  5. If clear, go back to 145.500 and say: "THEIR CALLSIGN, please QSY to 145.450"
  6. Both stations move — start your QSO
Practical Tip

145.450 (V36/S18) is about as conventional a QSY choice as exists. It's 4 channels below calling and almost always the first frequency people try. If it's busy, step down to 145.4375 (V35) or 145.425 (V34).

70cm Note

The 70cm (433 MHz) FM simplex range uses the same 12.5 kHz grid principle but with different channel designations. The calling frequency is 433.500 MHz. QSY to any clear 12.5 kHz-spaced channel in the 433.400–433.575 MHz simplex range.

2

VHF/UHF FM Repeater

Contacts through a local repeater — extends your range by retransmitting on another frequency

How it works: You don't need to call CQ on a repeater — just put your callsign out and see if anyone responds. Repeater contacts tend to be more casual than simplex.

Step 1 — Call on the repeater

Tune to the repeater output frequency. Set the correct CTCSS tone if required.
Say: "YOUR CALLSIGN listening through [repeater callsign, e.g., GB3XX]."

Or simply: "YOUR CALLSIGN listening."

Wait for a response. If nobody comes back, wait a few minutes and try again.

Step 2 — Someone responds

They say: "YOUR CALLSIGN, this is THEIR CALLSIGN. Good afternoon."

Step 3 — Have the conversation

Say: "THEIR CALLSIGN, this is YOUR CALLSIGN. Good afternoon. My name is [your name], QTH is [your location]. You're coming in [signal report] through the repeater. Over."

Continue the conversation naturally. Leave a gap between overs so other stations can break in if needed.

Step 4 — Close the contact

Say: "THEIR CALLSIGN, this is YOUR CALLSIGN. Thanks for the chat. 73 from YOUR CALLSIGN. Out."
Repeater Etiquette

Keep transmissions short — others may be waiting to use the repeater. Leave a 2-second gap between overs. If you hear a station calling "break" or "breaker", pause and let them in. Some repeaters have a timeout (typically 2–3 minutes) — if you talk too long, the repeater will drop your signal.

3

HF SSB (Phone)

HF bands — 80m, 40m, 20m, etc. — single sideband voice. Below 10 MHz use LSB; above 10 MHz use USB.

How it works: HF contacts are typically with more distant stations and follow a more structured format. Clear phonetics are essential — signals can fade.

Calling CQ

Find a clear frequency. Listen for 30 seconds. Ask "Is this frequency in use?"
Say: "CQ CQ CQ, this is YOUR CALLSIGN, YOUR CALLSIGN, YOUR CALLSIGN.
[Phonetics — e.g., Mike Seven Alpha Bravo Charlie].
Calling CQ and standing by."

Repeat two or three times. On HF, stations may be far away and signals can fade, so clear phonetics are essential.

When a station responds

They say: "YOUR CALLSIGN, this is THEIR CALLSIGN."

Your first over

Say: "THEIR CALLSIGN, this is YOUR CALLSIGN. Good afternoon and thanks for calling.
My name is [your name], name is [name].
My QTH is [your town/city, country].
Your signal report is [RS, e.g., five nine].
The rig here is [your radio, e.g., IC-7610] running [power, e.g., 25 watts] into a [antenna, e.g., dipole at 10 metres].
How do you copy? THEIR CALLSIGN, this is YOUR CALLSIGN. Over."
What's in a Typical HF Exchange

Name, location (QTH), signal report, rig and antenna, and power. This is the standard format — you'll hear it repeated across the bands. After the first exchange, the conversation can go anywhere: weather, equipment, conditions, other hobbies.

Closing

Say: "THEIR CALLSIGN, this is YOUR CALLSIGN. Thanks for the QSO, [their name].
I hope to catch you on the bands again. 73 and best wishes from [your location].
THEIR CALLSIGN, this is YOUR CALLSIGN. Out."
4

Responding to a CQ Call

When you hear another station calling CQ and want to respond

Often easier than calling CQ yourself — someone else has found a clear frequency and made the first move.

You hear a CQ call

You hear: "CQ CQ CQ, this is THEIR CALLSIGN..."

Respond

Wait until they finish calling, then say: "THEIR CALLSIGN, this is YOUR CALLSIGN, [phonetics]. Over."

That's it — short and clear. Don't give your callsign more than twice. If they don't hear you, wait for their next CQ call and try again.

If conditions are poor, you can add your location:

Alternative: "THEIR CALLSIGN, this is YOUR CALLSIGN, [phonetics], in [your location]. Over."
5

CW (Morse Code)

CW contacts follow a highly standardised format using Q-codes and abbreviations

Speed tip: If you're learning, call on the QRS (slow CW) centres of activity, or add "QRS" to your CQ call. Most operators will happily slow down for you.

Calling CQ

CQ CQ CQ DE YOUR CALLSIGN YOUR CALLSIGN YOUR CALLSIGN K

DE = "from". K = "go ahead."

When a station responds

YOUR CALLSIGN DE THEIR CALLSIGN THEIR CALLSIGN K

Standard CW exchange

THEIR CALLSIGN DE YOUR CALLSIGN
GM/GA/GE
UR RST 579 579
NAME IS [your name] [your name]
QTH IS [your location]
HW CPY? THEIR CALLSIGN DE YOUR CALLSIGN KN

KN = "go ahead, only the named station."

Closing

THEIR CALLSIGN DE YOUR CALLSIGN
TU FER QSO [their name]
HPE CUAGN 73 73
THEIR CALLSIGN DE YOUR CALLSIGN SK

TU = thank you. FER = for. HPE CUAGN = hope to see you again. SK = end of contact.

6

Digital Voice (D-STAR / DMR / Fusion)

Internet-linked repeaters or hotspots — can connect you worldwide via reflectors and talkgroups

D-STAR — Step 1: Select a reflector or gateway

Program your radio with the correct module (e.g., REF001C, XLX456A). Your callsign must be registered with the D-STAR gateway system.

D-STAR — Step 2: Call

Say: "Calling on [reflector name]. This is YOUR CALLSIGN, [phonetics]. Is anyone around? Over."

D-STAR — Step 3: Standard exchange

Same as FM simplex — name, location, signal report, rig. Mention you're using D-STAR and which hotspot/repeater you're going through.

DMR

DMR uses talkgroups rather than reflectors. Select the appropriate talkgroup (e.g., TG235 for UK, TG91 for worldwide). Key up briefly to activate the talkgroup on the repeater/hotspot, wait 2 seconds, then call.
Key Differences from Analogue

Wait for the "kerchunk" to clear — digital systems have a brief setup delay. Wait 2 seconds after keying up before speaking.

No need to QSY — talkgroups and reflectors are separate channels; you won't block other users unless you're on a busy talkgroup.

Registration required — D-STAR, DMR, and Fusion all require your callsign to be registered before you can transmit.

7

Nets and Scheduled Groups

Organised nets — a scheduled on-air meeting where a net controller manages the conversation

Checking in to a net

Net controller says: "This is [net controller callsign], net control for the [net name]. Any stations wishing to check in?"
Say: "YOUR CALLSIGN."

Just your callsign — keep it brief. The net controller will acknowledge you and add you to the list. Wait to be called before transmitting again.

When called by net control

Say: "[Net controller callsign], this is YOUR CALLSIGN. [Your traffic/comment]. Back to net control."
Net Etiquette

Follow the net controller's instructions. Don't transmit unless called. If you need to leave early, ask to be excused. Nets are a great way to practise — you know someone will be there at the scheduled time.

Signal Report Guide

On FM, tone is always 9 (clean), so we only give R (Readability) and S (Strength):

Readability (R)Meaning
1Unreadable
2Barely readable, occasional words
3Readable with considerable difficulty
4Readable with practically no difficulty
5Perfectly readable
Strength (S)Meaning
1Barely perceptible
3Weak
5Fairly good
7Moderately strong
9Very strong

Common reports: "5 and 9" (perfect), "5 and 7" (good), "3 and 5" (readable but not strong).

Be Honest

Giving everyone "5 and 9" regardless of actual signal quality is a common habit — but honest reports are more useful. If you can barely hear them, say so. They might adjust their antenna or power.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

MistakeWhy it's wrongWhat to do instead
"Over and out" Contradicts itself — "over" means "your turn", "out" means "finished" Use "over" during the QSO, "out" at the end
Calling CQ only twice Too short — stations may miss it Call CQ three times, give your callsign three times
Talking on the calling frequency Blocks others from making contact Move to a clear channel after initial contact
Not giving your callsign Legal requirement under licence conditions Give callsign at start, end, and every 15 minutes
Speaking too fast Especially on HF with fading signals Speak clearly at a moderate pace
"Breaker breaker" CB radio language, not amateur radio Use "break" if you need to interrupt
"73s" 73 already means "best wisheS" (plural) Just say "73"
Not listening first You might transmit over an existing QSO Always listen before transmitting
