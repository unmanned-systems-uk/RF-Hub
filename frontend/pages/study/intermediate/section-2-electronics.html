2A1

AC Mains Safety

Before the electronics — one safety rule that is always in the back of your mind.

Mains AC voltage in the UK is 230 V RMS. The peak voltage is actually 325 V.

  • Never work on live mains equipment.
  • Fit a fuse in the live (brown) wire — not the neutral.
  • An RCD (residual current device) protects you if current leaks to earth.
  • Double-insulated equipment has no earth connection by design — do not add one.
ℹ
2A1: You need to know that 230 V AC mains has a peak voltage significantly higher than 230 V. (Peak = 230 × √2 ≈ 325 V — √ means "square root of".)

Resistors, Capacitors, and Inductors

Resistors

A resistor opposes the flow of electric current. Resistance is measured in ohms (Ω, "omega").

2C1: The 4-band colour code. You must be able to read a resistor value from its bands, and identify the tolerance band.

Preferred values — the E12 series

Resistors are not made in every value. The E12 series has 12 values per decade: 10, 12, 15, 18, 22, 27, 33, 39, 47, 56, 68, 82 — then 100, 120, 150… These are the values you will find in catalogues and exam questions.

Resistors in series

When resistors are connected end-to-end, the resistances add.

R_total = R1 + R2 + R3 + ...

Example: 100 Ω + 150 Ω + 220 Ω = 470 Ω

Resistors in parallel

When resistors share the same two nodes, the combined resistance is less than the smallest individual resistor.

For two resistors:   R_total = (R1 × R2) / (R1 + R2)
For any number:      1/R_total = 1/R1 + 1/R2 + 1/R3 + ...
⚠
Common mistake: Students often add resistances in parallel instead of using the reciprocal formula. The parallel combination is ALWAYS less than the smallest resistor.

Example 1: 100 Ω in parallel with 150 Ω

R = (100 × 150) / (100 + 150) = 15 000 / 250 = 60 Ω

Example 2: Three resistors — 1.8 kΩ, 2.7 kΩ, 3.3 kΩ in parallel

1/R = 1/1800 + 1/2700 + 1/3300 = 0.000556 + 0.000370 + 0.000303 = 0.001229
R = 1 / 0.001229 = 813 Ω
ℹ
2C1: Parallel resistance is always less than the smallest resistor. Know both the 2-resistor shortcut and the general reciprocal formula.

Potential Dividers

A potential divider (voltage divider) uses two or more resistors in series to tap off a fraction of the supply voltage.

The output voltage is:

V_out = Vs × R2 / (R1 + R2)

This only works accurately if the load draws very little current — significant load current changes the effective R2 value.

ℹ
2C2: The potential divider formula. Questions often give you R1, R2, and Vs, and ask for V_out — or vice versa.

Example: In a potential divider, R1 = 1 kΩ and R2 = 4 kΩ across 10 V.
V_out = 10 × 4 000 / (1 000 + 4 000) = 10 × 4/5 = 8 V

Voltage in a series circuit

In a series circuit, the sum of voltage drops equals the supply voltage. Current is the same everywhere.

Example: R1 = 200 Ω, R2 = 600 Ω in series. Voltage across R1 = 4 V.

I = V_R1 / R1 = 4 / 200 = 20 mA
V_R2 = I × R2 = 20 mA × 600 = 12 V
Supply = 4 + 12 = 16 V
ℹ
2C3: In a series circuit, current is the same everywhere. Use this to find voltages across individual components.

Internal resistance and terminal voltage

Real batteries and power supplies have an internal resistance. Under load, the terminal voltage drops because current flows through this resistance.

V_terminal = V_open_circuit - (I × R_internal)

Example: Terminal voltage is 13.2 V at 1 A, and 12.4 V at 5 A.

ΔV = 13.2 - 12.4 = 0.8 V    ΔI = 5 - 1 = 4 A
R_internal = 0.8 / 4 = 0.2 Ω
V_oc = 13.2 + (1 × 0.2) = 13.4 V

Capacitors

A capacitor stores charge. It consists of two metal plates separated by an insulating material (the dielectric). Capacitance is measured in farads (F). Practical units: μF (μ, "mu" — 10⁻⁶ F), nF (10⁻⁹ F), pF (10⁻¹² F).

Capacitors in series and parallel

Capacitors combine the opposite way to resistors:

  • Parallel: C_total = C1 + C2 + C3 (capacitances add)
  • Series: 1/C_total = 1/C1 + 1/C2 + ... (reciprocal formula)
⚠
Common mistake: Many students mix up series/parallel rules for C vs R. For capacitors, parallel = add. For resistors, series = add.

Inductors

An inductor is a coil of wire with multiple turns. It stores energy in a magnetic field. Inductance is measured in henries (H) — practical values are mH or μH.

Inductance increases if you: add more turns; use a ferrite or iron core; increase cross-sectional area; reduce the spacing between turns (turns closer together = higher inductance).

Inductance decreases if you: remove turns; pull turns apart; remove the core.

Inductors in series: L_total = L1 + L2 + ...  ·  Inductors in parallel: 1/L_total = 1/L1 + 1/L2 + ...

ℹ
2D4: An inductor is a coil of wire with several turns. Inductance depends on number of turns, core material, and turn spacing. 2D6: Reducing turn spacing increases inductance.

The corkscrew rule — a memory aid for the direction of the magnetic field: curl your right hand around the wire with your thumb pointing in the direction of conventional current flow; your fingers curl in the direction of the magnetic field lines. (Same principle as a corkscrew rotating clockwise as it drives forward into a cork.) In a coil, this means the direction of current flow determines which end acts as the magnetic "north" pole — and swapping the current direction swaps the poles.

Resistivity and Conductor Resistance (2D6)

The resistance of a conductor depends not just on Ohm’s law but on the physical properties of the material it is made from and its dimensions.

Resistivity (symbol ρ, Greek letter rho) is a property of the material itself — it measures how strongly a material opposes the flow of electric current per unit dimensions.

R = ρ × l / A

Where:
  R = resistance (Ω)
  ρ = resistivity of the material (Ω·m)
  l = length of the conductor (m)
  A = cross-sectional area of the conductor (m²)

Rearranging: ρ = R × A / l

  • A longer conductor has higher resistance (proportional)
  • A wider conductor (larger cross-section) has lower resistance (inversely proportional)
  • A material with lower ρ is a better conductor
MaterialResistivity ρ (Ω·m)Notes
Silver1.6 × 10−8Best common conductor; expensive
Copper1.7 × 10−8Used for virtually all RF wire and cable
Aluminium2.8 × 10−8Lighter than copper; used in HV power lines
Iron / Steel~1 × 10−7Much worse conductor; avoid for RF work

Copper wire has very low resistivity, but long thin feeders still have significant resistance at DC and at RF (where skin effect concentrates current in the outer skin of the conductor, further increasing effective resistance). This is why coaxial cable loss increases with frequency.

ℹ
2D6: Resistivity (ρ) is a property of the material. Conductor resistance R = ρl/A — longer and thinner conductors have higher resistance; better conductor materials have lower ρ. Copper (ρ ≈ 1.7 × 10−8 Ω·m) is the standard choice for RF wiring.

R = (470 × 680) / (470 + 680) = 319 600 / 1 150 ≈ 278 Ω

Always less than the smallest resistor (470 Ω) — eliminate any answer above 470 Ω.

Yellow = 4, Violet = 7, Red = ×100, Gold = ±5%

Q3. In a potential divider, R1 = 1 kΩ and R2 = 4 kΩ, connected across 10 V. What is the output voltage measured across R2?

V_out = 10 × 4 000 / (1 000 + 4 000) = 10 × 4/5 = 8 V

Q4. A polarised capacitor is installed backwards in a circuit. What is likely to happen?

It can fail — potentially bulging, leaking, or rupturing. Always fit polarised capacitors with correct polarity — positive terminal to the more positive supply rail.

Q5. How can the inductance of an air-core coil be increased without adding turns?

Insert a ferrite or iron core. This concentrates the magnetic field and increases inductance significantly — by a factor related to the core's permeability.

2D

Ohm's Law and Power

Ohm's Law

Ohm's law: Voltage equals current multiplied by resistance. Three versions — rearrange as needed:

V = I × R    (voltage)
I = V / R    (current)
R = V / I    (resistance)

Units: V = volts · I = amperes (often mA or μA) · R = ohms (Ω)

ℹ
2D1: Ohm's law is the single most used equation in electronics. Learn all three rearrangements. Unit conversions: 1 mA = 0.001 A, 1 kΩ = 1 000 Ω.

Example: A 1.5 kΩ resistor has 6 V across it. What current flows?

I = V / R = 6 / 1 500 = 0.004 A = 4 mA

Example: A current of 50 mA flows through a 100 Ω resistor. What voltage is across it?

V = I × R = 0.05 × 100 = 5 V

Every SI prefix is exactly 3 powers of ten apart — each step is ×1 000. Going up from pico: p → n → μ → m → (base) → k → M → G. The exponents are 3, 6, 9, 12 — just like the song. Move right = ×1 000 each time. Move left = ÷1 000 each time.
⚠
Unit errors are the most common calculation mistake. Always convert to base units (A and Ω) before calculating. Or be careful that mA × kΩ = V (the thousands cancel).

Power

Power is the rate of energy use, measured in watts (W). Three power formulas — derived from P = V × I and Ohm's law:

P = V × I      (power from voltage and current)
P = I² × R    (power from current and resistance)
P = V² / R    (power from voltage and resistance)

(² means "squared" — the value multiplied by itself; e.g. I² = I × I)

ℹ
The squared relationship — doubling voltage quadruples power. Because P = V2/R, if you double the voltage across a fixed resistance, the power goes up by 22 = four times, not two times. This is why transmitting at 100 W into an antenna produces four times the radiated power of transmitting at 50 W (assuming the same antenna and feeder). Same reasoning in reverse: dropping to 25 W is a 4× power cut, not 2×.
ℹ
2D2: Know all three power formulas. Identify which two quantities you are given (V and R, V and I, or I and R) and pick the matching formula.

Example: What power is dissipated in a 50 Ω resistor with 10 V across it?

P = V² / R = 100 / 50 = 2 W

Example: A 100 mA current flows through a 470 Ω resistor. What power is dissipated?

P = I² × R = (0.1)² × 470 = 0.01 × 470 = 4.7 W

Practical note — power ratings: Resistors are rated for how much power they can handle. Common ratings: ¼ W, ½ W, 1 W, 2 W. Exceeding the rating causes the resistor to burn out. If a resistor will dissipate 1.5 W, choose at least a 2 W rated component.

Component Properties (2D syllabus items)

Capacitor construction (2D1)

A capacitor is made of two metal plates separated by an insulating material (the dielectric). The dielectric can be ceramic, polyester, polypropylene, electrolytic paste, or air.

Crystals — series vs parallel resonance (2D5)

Quartz crystals are used to control oscillator frequency with high accuracy. A crystal exhibits two slightly different resonant frequencies:

  • Series resonance: lower frequency, acts as low impedance at resonance
  • Parallel resonance: slightly higher frequency, acts as high impedance
ℹ
2D5: Crystal oscillators — always check whether the circuit specifies series or parallel resonance before ordering a replacement crystal. Using the wrong type shifts the operating frequency.

2E

Alternating Current

AC versus DC

Direct current (DC): flows in one direction only. A battery produces DC.

Alternating current (AC): reverses direction periodically. Mains electricity is AC. A sine wave is the purest form of AC.

AC Waveforms (2E2)

A sine wave is described by:

  • Frequency (f): how many complete cycles per second — unit: hertz (Hz), also kHz, MHz, GHz.
  • Period (T, "tee"): the time for one complete cycle — unit: seconds (s).
f = 1 / T      T = 1 / f

Other waveform shapes: radio work is dominated by sine waves, but you'll meet square waves (constant amplitude at fixed high/low levels, used in digital logic and switching supplies) and triangle waves (linear ramps up and down) in test equipment and digital circuits. The full theory of these non-sinusoidal shapes belongs to the Full Licence — for the Intermediate exam, just recognise the names and know that any non-sine wave can be built up as a sum of sine waves at the fundamental frequency plus harmonics.

Example: Mains frequency = 50 Hz → Period = 1 / 50 = 0.02 s (20 ms)

  • Peak voltage (V_pk): the maximum voltage reached in one direction.
  • Peak-to-peak voltage (V_pp): total swing from positive to negative peak. V_pp = 2 × V_pk
  • RMS voltage (V_rms): the DC equivalent in terms of heating effect.
V_rms = V_pk / √2  ≈  V_pk × 0.707
V_pk  = V_rms × √2 ≈  V_rms × 1.414
ℹ
2E3: RMS stands for Root Mean Square. The RMS value of an AC signal is the DC voltage that would produce the same heating effect in a resistor. UK mains: V_rms = 230 V, V_pk = 230 × 1.414 ≈ 325 V.
⚠
2E3: Which signal causes more heating in a 50 Ω load — 10 V DC or 10 V AC (peak)? Answer: 10 V DC. The AC peak is 10 V, but its RMS is only 10 / 1.414 ≈ 7.07 V. DC wins.

Capacitors and AC (2E1)

A capacitor blocks DC but allows AC to pass. With DC, the capacitor charges to the supply voltage and no more current flows. With AC, the voltage is always changing, so the capacitor is always charging or discharging — current appears to flow through it.

This makes capacitors useful for: Coupling (pass AC signal between stages, block DC bias) · Decoupling (bypass AC to ground) · Filtering (pass high frequencies, block low).

ℹ
2E1: A capacitor will allow AC to flow but block DC. This is a fundamental property with many practical uses.

Charging and discharging — the RC and L/R curves

When a capacitor charges through a resistor from a DC supply, the voltage across it rises exponentially — fast at first, then slowing as it approaches the supply voltage. Discharging is the mirror image: fast at first, then slowing as it approaches zero. An inductor's current behaves the same way — rises exponentially to a steady value when switched on, decays exponentially when switched off (though back-EMF makes the switch-off dramatic if there's no discharge path).

The time constant is a critical concept for filter design, keyclick suppression, and rise-time calculations — you'll meet it again in the filter module and in the CW keying discussion in section 3.

Reactance (2E4 and 2E5)

Reactance is the opposition to AC offered by a capacitor or inductor. Measured in ohms (Ω).

Capacitive reactance (Xc — "ex sub C")

Xc = 1 / (2π × f × C)

(π, "pi" ≈ 3.14159 — the mathematical constant that appears in all reactance and resonance formulas)

  • Xc decreases as frequency increases → capacitors pass high frequencies easily
  • Xc increases as frequency decreases → capacitors block low frequencies and DC

Inductive reactance (XL — "ex sub L")

XL = 2π × f × L
  • XL increases as frequency increases → inductors oppose high frequencies
  • XL decreases as frequency decreases → inductors pass low frequencies easily

Phase relationships (ELI the ICE man)

Reactive components introduce a 90° phase shift between voltage and current:

  • ELI — in an inductor (L), E (voltage) leads I (current) by 90°. The back-EMF opposes the change in current, so current lags.
  • ICE — in a capacitor (C), I (current) leads E (voltage) by 90°. Current flows first to charge the plates; the voltage builds up behind.

Reactance is Ohm's law with a twist

For a pure reactance, magnitude of V/I gives reactance in ohms — exactly like Ohm's law:

R  = V / I           (resistor, in phase)
Xc = Vrms / Irms     (capacitor, 90° phase shift)
XL = Vrms / Irms   (inductor, 90° phase shift)
Z  = V / I           (general case — magnitude of impedance)

All in ohms. Reactance and impedance behave like resistance for magnitude calculations — but they carry a phase shift that pure resistance does not.

ℹ
2E4: Reactance is measured in ohms — it is the ratio of voltage to current in a reactive component, frequency-dependent. 2E5/2E6: Reactance (X) and resistance (R) combine to give impedance (Z) = √(R² + X²).

Self-Check — 2E

Q1. An AC signal has a peak voltage of 14.1 V. What is its RMS voltage?

V_rms = 14.1 / √2 = 14.1 / 1.414 ≈ 10 V RMS

Q2. A signal at 50.150 MHz is in which amateur band?

λ = 300 / 50.150 ≈ 6 m → 6 m band (50 – 52 MHz)

Q3. Why does a capacitor block DC but pass AC?

With DC, the capacitor charges to the supply voltage and then no further current flows. With AC, the voltage continuously reverses so the capacitor continuously charges/discharges — current effectively passes through it.

Q4. A 10 V DC signal and a 10 V peak AC signal are applied separately to identical 50 Ω resistors. Which produces more heat?

The 10 V DC produces more heat. The 10 V peak AC has an RMS of only 7.07 V. Power from DC = 10² / 50 = 2 W. Power from AC = 7.07² / 50 ≈ 1 W.

Q5. What is the approximate length of a half-wave dipole for 14.2 MHz?

λ = 300 / 14.2 = 21.1 m. Half-wave = 10.6 m. In practice a physical dipole is about 95% of calculated, so about 10 m.

Impedance, Resonance, and Q Factor

Impedance (2E6)

Impedance (Z, "zed") is the total opposition to AC in a circuit. It combines resistance (R) and reactance (X).

Z = √(R² + X²)     (ohms)
  • If only resistance: Z = R
  • If only reactance: Z = X
  • If both: Z is always greater than either R or X alone

Recall Z = V/I from the reactance section — impedance is the general case combining R and X in the same V/I form as Ohm's law.

Impedance is used when describing the input or output of RF circuits, antennas, and feeders. A typical coaxial feeder has an impedance of 50 Ω.

Tuned Circuits (2H1 and 2H3)

A tuned circuit consists of an inductor (L) and a capacitor (C) connected together. Energy bounces between the magnetic field of the inductor and the electric field of the capacitor. At the resonant frequency, the inductive reactance equals the capacitive reactance (XL = XC).

f₀ = 1 / (2π × √(L × C))

(f₀, "f-nought" — the subscript ₀ denotes the resonant or natural value of f)

ℹ
2H3: Energy can transfer between a capacitor and an inductor at the resonant frequency. At resonance, XL = XC.

Off resonance, XL ≠ XC — one reactance dominates the other. Below f₀: XC is high and XL is low, so capacitors block and inductors pass. Above f₀: the roles reverse — XL is high and XC is low. This imbalance is exactly how filters work — by choosing which of L or C sits in series with the signal path and which shunts to ground, you build low-pass, high-pass, band-pass or band-stop behaviour.

How the tank oscillates — energy sloshing between L and C

The magic of a tuned circuit is that energy alternates between two forms: the electric field in the capacitor and the magnetic field in the inductor. Left alone, one full swing of the cycle repeats every 1/f₀ seconds — that's the resonant frequency.

  1. Cap charged, inductor idle. All the circuit's energy sits in the capacitor's electric field. No current flows yet.
  2. Cap discharges through L. The capacitor pushes current through the inductor. Energy flows from the electric field into the inductor's growing magnetic field.
  3. Cap empty, all energy in L. Cap voltage is zero. Current is at maximum. All the energy now lives in the inductor's magnetic field.
  4. L's field collapses, recharging cap. An inductor resists change in current — as the current tries to fall, the collapsing magnetic field induces an EMF that keeps current flowing, dumping energy back into the capacitor with opposite polarity.
  5. And back again. Cap discharges the other way, L stores the field, then re-charges the cap back to its original polarity. That completes one full cycle at f₀.

Sustaining oscillation — pushing at the natural frequency

Real tuned circuits have resistive losses — principally wire resistance in the inductor and dielectric loss in the capacitor. Each cycle a small amount of energy escapes as heat, so the oscillation damps out — the amplitude decays exponentially. Higher Q means slower decay.

To keep the tank swinging, an oscillator circuit feeds in small energy pulses at exactly f₀. Push a child on a swing at the natural frequency and each shove adds to the motion; push at any other rhythm and the shoves fight the swing. Same principle — the tank readily accepts energy at its resonant frequency and rejects it at any other.

Where you meet each filter in a station:

  • Low-pass (LPF) on the transmitter output — passes the band, rejects harmonics that would radiate on higher bands.
  • High-pass (HPF) in a receiver's front end — blocks strong broadcast-band signals that would overload the RX.
  • Band-pass (BPF) — selects a specific band and rejects everything else (IF filters, preselectors).
  • Band-stop / notch — rejects one specific frequency (broadcast breakthrough, a birdie inside the passband).

Cut-off frequency and the -3 dB point

Every filter's cut-off frequency is defined at the half-power point. Filter response curves usually plot voltage (easier to measure than power), and because P ∝ V², a voltage drop to 0.707 × Vin means the power has halved.

V_out = 0.707 × V_in   →   power = ½ × input power   →   -3 dB

So when you see a filter response curve labelled "-3 dB point" or "corner frequency", it's the same thing: the frequency at which the filter has attenuated the signal by half in power terms. The dB scale itself is covered in more depth in the RF measurements section.

ℹ
Why the hump at cutoff? In low-pass and high-pass modes, you may notice the response exceeds 0 dB near the cutoff frequency — the signal briefly becomes louder than the input. This is resonance — the same f₀ = 1/(2π√LC) at work.

At resonance, the inductor and capacitor exchange energy back and forth. With low R (high Q), that energy exchange creates a voltage peak before the filter rolls off. Increase R and the hump flattens out.

All four filter modes are the same LC resonance in different circuit topologies:
• Bandpass — resonance is the peak you want (passes f₀)
• Band-stop — resonance is the notch (rejects f₀)
• Low-pass — resonance appears as a hump just before cutoff
• High-pass — resonance appears as a hump just after cutoff

Series tuned circuit

At resonance: impedance is minimum (theoretically zero; in practice just the winding resistance). Current is maximum. Used as a bandpass filter.

ℹ
2H1: At resonance, a series tuned circuit has minimum impedance.

Parallel tuned circuit

At resonance: impedance is maximum (theoretically infinite). Current in the external circuit is minimum. Used as a band-stop filter or to select a frequency in an oscillator.

Simple oscillator — parallel tank + amplifier feedback

The classic recipe for a variable-frequency local oscillator (LO): a parallel LC tank sets the frequency, and a small amount of the amplifier's output is fed back into the tank to replace the energy lost to resistance each cycle. The tank oscillates freely at f₀ — the amplifier just tops up the energy.

Fixed L, variable C: in vintage receivers, the inductor is a fixed coil and the capacitor is a mechanical open-air variable capacitor — interleaved metal vanes rotated by the tuning knob. Turning the knob changes C, which changes f₀ = 1/(2π√LC) — that's how you tune across a band. In modern radios the mechanical cap is usually replaced by a varicap (voltage-controlled semiconductor cap) or a synthesised digital LO (PLL/DDS), but the underlying principle is the same parallel resonance.

Varying L instead of C: the inductor can also be made variable — a roller coaster inductor uses a spindle that rolls a contact along the coil turns (used in HF antenna tuners), and a slug tuner screws a ferrite core in and out of the coil, changing its inductance. Whether you vary L or C, the result is the same: f₀ shifts along f₀ = 1/(2π√LC).

Antenna traps — parallel resonance in the wire

An antenna trap is a parallel LC circuit inserted into an antenna wire or element. At the trap's resonant frequency, it presents very high impedance — the antenna behaves as if the wire is cut at that point. Below resonance, the trap acts mostly as an inductor, extending the electrical length of the wire.

How this enables multi-band operation: a trap dipole for 40 m and 80 m has a pair of traps resonant at ~7 MHz. On 40 m, the traps "open" the wire at the trap position — the antenna resonates as a shorter 40 m dipole. On 80 m, the traps pass current through and act as loading coils, letting the full wire length resonate on 80 m. One antenna, two bands, no switching.

High voltage and current at resonance

At resonance, voltage and circulating current inside a tuned circuit can be much higher than the supply voltage.

Key filter terms

  • Cut-off frequency (fc): the point where response has fallen 3 dB below the passband level (the "-3 dB point").
  • Passband: the range of frequencies a filter lets through with little attenuation.
  • Stopband: the range of frequencies a filter attenuates heavily.
  • Centre frequency (f₀): the middle of a band-pass or band-stop filter's response.
  • Bandwidth (BW): the width of the passband (or stopband), usually measured at the -3 dB points.
  • Q: selectivity, Q = f₀ / BW — covered in detail just below.
  • Insertion loss: how much a filter attenuates its own passband (ideally 0 dB; real filters always lose a little).
  • Roll-off / order: how steeply the response falls outside the passband. A higher-order filter rolls off faster (steeper skirts) but costs more components.

Q Factor

Q (Quality factor) describes how selective a tuned circuit is.

Q = f₀ / BW

Where BW is the bandwidth (range of frequencies within 3 dB of peak).

  • High Q: narrow bandwidth, sharp tuning, more selective
  • Low Q: wide bandwidth, less selective

Q depends on the losses in the circuit — mostly the resistance of the inductor winding. A good-quality air-core or silver-plated coil gives high Q.

Digital Signals and Sampling (2F1)

Analogue signals can be converted to digital data: sample at regular intervals → quantise each sample → encode as binary numbers.

Nyquist theorem

To accurately represent an analogue signal, the sampling rate must be at least twice the highest frequency in the signal.

f_sample ≥ 2 × f_max    (the Nyquist rate)

Example: An audio signal up to 4 kHz. Minimum sample rate = 2 × 4 kHz = 8 kHz.

Sampling below the Nyquist rate causes aliasing — false frequencies appear in the output.

ℹ
2F1: A higher-resolution digital representation requires more binary bits. SDR (Software Defined Radio) uses ADC to digitise the signal early, then processes it all in software.

Semiconductors — Diodes, Transistors, and Transformers

Transformers (2G1)

A transformer uses electromagnetic induction to transfer energy between two circuits. It consists of two coils (primary and secondary) wound on a shared magnetic core.

  • Transformers only work with AC. DC does not create a changing magnetic field, so no energy is transferred to the secondary.
  • The iron or ferrite core concentrates the magnetic field, increasing coupling and improving efficiency.
  • The voltage ratio equals the turns ratio:
V_primary / V_secondary = N_primary / N_secondary
ℹ
2G1: A transformer can only be used with AC. The iron core concentrates the magnetic field. The turns ratio determines the voltage ratio.
⚠
Do not confuse the turns ratio direction: more turns on primary → step down. More turns on secondary → step up.

Example: Primary = 3 turns, secondary = 1 turn. Primary voltage = 240 V AC.

240 / V_sec = 3 / 1 → V_sec = 240 / 3 = 80 V AC

Impedance transformation: Transformers also transform impedance. The impedance ratio equals the square of the turns ratio.

Z_primary / Z_secondary = (N_primary / N_secondary)²

Not just for mains AC: the impedance transformation above is why transformers appear throughout radio equipment.

  • Mains PSUs: step down 240 V AC to the low-voltage AC needed for rectification.
  • Isolation: galvanically separate two circuits (safety, breaking hum loops).
  • Antenna matching: baluns (balanced-to-unbalanced, e.g. dipole to coax) and ununs (unbalanced-to-unbalanced, e.g. end-fed wire to 50 Ω coax) are ferrite-cored RF transformers.
  • RF stages: tuned transformers couple IF amplifier stages; matching transformers in transmitter output networks.

Semiconductor materials

Conductors (copper, aluminium) pass electric current easily — their atoms have loosely-bound electrons that drift when a voltage is applied. Insulators (glass, plastic) block current — their electrons are tightly bound. Semiconductors sit between the two: their conductivity can be controlled by adding tiny amounts of other elements.

The most common semiconductor is silicon. Pure silicon has fairly high resistance and isn't very useful on its own. It becomes useful once it is doped — small amounts of a different element are mixed in during manufacture:

  • N-type silicon is doped with atoms that donate spare electrons. It has an excess of mobile electrons — the charge carriers.
  • P-type silicon is doped with atoms that leave a shortage of electrons in the crystal. The missing electrons are called holes, which behave as if they were positive charge carriers.

When a piece of P-type silicon is joined to a piece of N-type silicon, the result is a P-N junction — the basis of every diode, transistor, LED and IC. At the boundary, electrons from the N side diffuse across and fill holes on the P side, forming a thin depletion layer that carries no free charge — an internal insulator that gives the junction its useful behaviour.

ℹ
Historical note on current direction: current is really the flow of electrons from the negative to the positive terminal (electrons carry negative charge and are attracted to positive). Early physicists assumed the opposite direction — this legacy convention, called conventional current, is still used in circuit diagrams and arrows on components like diodes.

Diodes (2I1)

A diode allows current to flow in one direction only. It is a semiconductor P–N junction. The arrow in the symbol points in the direction of conventional current flow (anode → cathode).

Key applications:

  • Rectification: converting AC to DC (diode conducts on positive half-cycles only)
  • Relay flyback protection: diode across a relay coil prevents voltage spikes when the coil switches off
  • Signal detection: small signal diodes in detectors and mixers
ℹ
2I1: A diode is used to change alternating current to direct current. The diode only conducts when its anode is more positive than its cathode. Silicon forward voltage drop ≈ 0.6–0.7 V.

Relay flyback protection: When a relay coil is de-energised, it produces a large back-EMF voltage spike. A diode across the coil (cathode to positive supply) clamps this spike and protects the driving transistor.

Germanium diodes: an older semiconductor material with a much lower forward voltage drop of about 0.25 V (silicon is 0.6–0.7 V). Because they conduct at very small voltages, germanium diodes are used in receiver detector circuits where the incoming RF signal may be only a few tens of millivolts — a silicon diode wouldn't switch on at all. Rare in modern radios but still found in crystal-set and simple detector designs.

Bipolar Junction Transistors (BJT)

A BJT has three terminals: Base (B) — control input · Collector (C) — current flows in · Emitter (E) — current flows out. There are two types: NPN (more common) and PNP. The Intermediate syllabus only examines the NPN type — PNP works on the same principles with polarities reversed.

Current relationships in NPN:

IC = β × IB        (collector current = gain × base current)
IE = IC + IB       (emitter current = collector + base)
β  = IC / IB       (current gain)

A typical transistor has β ("beta") of 50–300.

ℹ
2I3: The current gain β of a transistor is defined as IC / IB.

Base-emitter forward drop: the base-emitter junction of a BJT is a P-N junction just like a diode, so it needs about 0.6 V of forward bias before any collector current flows at all. Below 0.6 V between base and emitter, the transistor is fully cut off.

Example: A transistor has β = 100. Base current = 50 μA. What is the collector current?

IC = β × IB = 100 × 0.00005 = 5 mA

Common emitter configuration (2I4)

The most common amplifier configuration. Input at base, output from collector, emitter is common. Characteristics: moderate-to-high voltage gain, high current gain, 180° phase shift, used for audio and RF amplifier stages.

Transistor biasing (2I4 and 2I5)

Biasing provides the correct DC voltages and currents to set the transistor's operating point. Without correct bias, the transistor either cuts off or saturates.

For linear amplification (a faithful, larger copy of the input signal), the transistor must sit in its linear operating region. The classic rule: set the DC output voltage to half the supply voltage. That gives the maximum symmetrical voltage swing — the output can rise or fall equally before clipping against the supply rail (top) or ground (bottom). Bias too high or too low and the signal clips on one side, causing distortion. Coupling capacitors on the input and output block the DC bias from reaching the rest of the circuit while passing the AC signal through.

⚠
β manufacturing tolerance: the current gain β of a given transistor type has a wide manufacturing spread — a "β = 100" part might actually be anywhere from ~50 to ~300. Robust amplifier designs use resistor networks that make the bias point insensitive to β, so the circuit works with any part from the batch.

Amplifier classes describe how much of the input signal cycle the transistor conducts for. The class determines the trade-off between linearity (faithful reproduction of the signal) and efficiency (how much DC power is converted to useful output vs wasted as heat). This matters in radio because transmitter PA stages must balance signal quality against power efficiency.

ClassConducts forEfficiencyUse
Class AFull cycleLowLinear/small-signal amplifiers

ℹ
2I4 / 2I5: Class A = full cycle, most linear. Class C = less than half cycle, most efficient, used for RF power. Class C is NOT suitable for linear amplification.

Think of it this way: Class A is like an engine running all the time — smooth but wasteful. Class C is like an engine that only fires brief pulses — efficient, but the output needs a tuned circuit to reconstruct the full waveform.

Field Effect Transistors (FET) (2H4 and 2H5)

A FET has three terminals: Gate (G) — control · Drain (D) — current in · Source (S) — current out.

ℹ
2H5: A FET is preferred over a bipolar transistor at the input of a receiver because it has a lower noise figure and higher input impedance.

Varicap diode (variable capacitance diode): A special diode where capacitance is controlled by the reverse bias voltage. More reverse voltage → smaller capacitance. Less reverse voltage → larger capacitance. Used in electronically tuned circuits (VCOs, AFC).

ℹ
2H4: The capacitance of a varicap diode is normally adjusted by varying the reverse bias voltage.

Power Supplies

Rectification (2I1)

Rectification converts alternating current (AC) to direct current (DC). A diode is the component used — it only conducts in one direction.

Half-wave rectifier

A single diode only passes the positive half-cycles. Output is a series of positive pulses with gaps. Simple but wasteful.

Full-wave rectifier with centre-tapped transformer

Two diodes plus a centre-tapped transformer gives full-wave rectification without needing four diodes. Each diode conducts on one half-cycle, so both halves of the input reach the output as positive pulses. More efficient than half-wave (no wasted half-cycle), but requires a special transformer with a tap at the centre of the secondary winding. Modern PSUs usually use the bridge rectifier below instead, which needs no centre tap.

Full-wave bridge rectifier

Four diodes arranged in a bridge pass both half-cycles. Both positive and negative half-cycles are converted to positive pulses. Output has twice the ripple frequency of half-wave. More efficient.

ℹ
2I1: A diode changes AC to DC. A full-wave bridge uses four diodes and is more efficient than a half-wave rectifier.

Smoothing (2I2 / 2J3)

After rectification, the output is pulsating DC. A smoothing capacitor reduces this ripple.

  • The capacitor charges to the peak voltage on each cycle
  • Between peaks, it discharges slowly through the load
  • Larger capacitor = less ripple = smoother DC
ℹ
2J3: The component that provides smoothing in a mains power supply is a capacitor. It stores charge between peaks to maintain the DC output level.

The residual variation is called ripple voltage. A large electrolytic capacitor (1 000 μF or more) is typically used in mains PSUs.

Voltage Regulation (2I5)

A voltage regulator keeps the output at a fixed voltage regardless of load.

Zener diode regulator

A zener diode is designed to break down at a specific reverse voltage (the zener voltage), maintaining a constant voltage across itself as long as some minimum current flows.

Series-pass regulator with error amplifier

A practical linear regulator adds a series-pass transistor that sits in-line with the load and handles the full load current. A second transistor and a Zener diode form an error amplifier: they compare the output voltage against the Zener reference and continuously adjust the series-pass transistor to keep the output constant even as load current changes. This is the circuit inside a typical linear voltage regulator IC (7805, 7812, etc.). Linear regulators produce clean output with little RF noise, but the series-pass transistor dissipates the excess voltage as heat — hence the need for heat sinks and the "inefficient and heavy" reputation of linear PSUs.

IC voltage regulators

Integrated circuit regulators (e.g. 7805 for +5 V, 7812 for +12 V) contain a complete regulation circuit in a single package. More precise and efficient than a simple zener.

ℹ
2I5: IC voltage regulators (integrated circuits) perform most regulation functions and are common in commercial power supplies. The 78xx series outputs +xx volts (e.g. 7812 → +12 V).

Batteries and Capacity (2J1 and 2J2)

Battery capacity is measured in ampere-hours (Ah) or milliampere-hours (mAh).

Capacity (Ah) = Current (A) × Time (hours)
ℹ
2J1: The Ah rating is an indication of the stored energy when fully charged. A 10 Ah battery can supply 10 A for 1 hour, or 1 A for 10 hours.

Combining cells — series adds voltage, parallel adds capacity

A single cell (e.g. an AA) produces a fixed voltage determined by its chemistry — ~1.5 V for a standard alkaline cell, ~1.2 V for a rechargeable NiMH, ~3.7 V for a lithium-ion. To get a different voltage or more capacity, cells are combined into a battery:

  • Cells in series — voltages add, capacity stays the same as one cell. Three 1.5 V AA cells in series give 4.5 V at the same mAh rating as a single cell.
  • Cells in parallel — voltage stays the same as one cell, capacities add. Two 1.5 V AA cells in parallel give 1.5 V at double the mAh rating.
  • Series-parallel combinations give both (e.g. a 12 V drill battery is typically 10× Li-ion cells wired as 3-series × multiple-parallel groups).

The choice depends on the load: high-current transceivers need parallel groups to sustain the current draw; multi-cell voltages (12 V, 18 V, 24 V) are built by series stacks.

Example: An SDR receiver needs 6 V at 350 mA for 25 hours.

Capacity = 0.35 A × 25 h = 8.75 Ah → choose next size up: 10 Ah

Integrated Circuits (2I6)

An integrated circuit (IC) contains many transistors, resistors, and sometimes capacitors all built into a single silicon chip. ICs allow complex circuits to be implemented in a tiny, reliable, inexpensive package. Common packages: DIP, TO-92, SOT-23 (surface mount).

ICs you may meet in amateur radio kit-building:

  • LM386 — a small audio power amplifier IC, used in many receiver audio stages and hobby projects.
  • NE602 / NE612 — an oscillator + mixer IC in one package, commonly used in direct-conversion receivers and simple superhet designs.
  • 78xx series — the linear voltage regulators covered above (7805 = 5 V, 7812 = 12 V, etc.).
  • Atmega328p — the microcontroller inside the Arduino Uno; countless amateur projects use it for keyer decoders, band monitors, rig controllers.
ℹ
2I6: An integrated circuit (IC) contains an entire circuit — transistors, resistors, etc. — on a single chip of silicon.

Decibels and Signal Levels

What is a Decibel? (2J2)

The decibel (dB) is a logarithmic unit for expressing a ratio of two power levels, or two voltage/current levels.

Why logarithmic? Signal levels in radio systems span an enormous range (from picowatts to kilowatts). Logarithms turn multiplication into addition — cascade gains and losses by adding dB values.

Power ratio in dB

2J2: +3 dB = double power, −3 dB = half power. +10 dB = 10× power. These are the values you must know without calculation.

Example: An amplifier has a power gain of 100. What is this in dB?

dB = 10 × log₁₀ (100) = 10 × 2 = 20 dB

Example: A filter has 30 dB of attenuation. By what factor is the power reduced?

30 dB = 10 + 10 + 10 → ×10 × ×10 × ×10 = ×1 000

Voltage ratio in dB

dB = 20 × log₁₀ (V_out / V_in)

Note the factor of 20 (not 10) for voltage ratios.

dBm and Absolute Signal Levels

dBm is decibels relative to 1 milliwatt. It is an absolute power level, not a ratio.

dBm = 10 × log₁₀ (Power in mW / 1 mW)

Cascaded Gains and Losses (2J3)

When signals pass through multiple stages, gains and losses in dB are added together.

Example: A receive chain has: −3 dB cable loss, +20 dB amplifier gain, −3 dB splitter loss.

Total: −3 + 20 − 3 = +14 dB net gain
ℹ
2J3: Add dB values for cascaded stages. A gain of +20 dB followed by −3 dB gives a net gain of +17 dB.

Signal-to-Noise Ratio (2J4)

Signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) describes how much stronger the signal is compared to the background noise, measured in dB. Higher SNR = better quality signal.

A receiver with a low noise figure (NF) adds less noise to the signal, giving a higher output SNR. This is why low-noise amplifiers (LNAs) are used as the first stage in receivers.

ℹ
2J4: Noise figure describes how much noise a receiver adds to the signal. A lower noise figure means better receiver performance.

Self-Check — 2J

Q1. An amplifier has a power gain of 1 000. Express this in dB.

dB = 10 × log₁₀ (1 000) = 10 × 3 = 30 dB

Q2. A 100 W transmitter output is reduced by 3 dB by a cable loss. What power reaches the antenna?

−3 dB = half power. 100 / 2 = 50 W

Q3. A receive chain has: LNA +20 dB gain · Filter −2 dB loss · Coax cable −5 dB loss. What is the total gain?

+20 − 2 − 5 = +13 dB net gain

Q4. A signal of +40 dBm passes through a 20 dB attenuator. What is the output signal level?

+40 − 20 = +20 dBm (which is 100 mW)

Q5. Two receivers: one has a noise figure of 3 dB, the other 8 dB. Which is better for weak signals?

The receiver with the 3 dB noise figure — it adds less noise, giving a better signal-to-noise ratio for weak signals.

★ KEY FACTS

Key Facts Summary

This section covers ~30% of the Intermediate exam. Prioritise the calculation topics: Ohm's law, power, parallel resistance, dB, and the Nyquist theorem.

  • Resistors in parallel: result is always LESS than the smallest
  • Capacitors in parallel: capacitances ADD (like resistors in series)
  • Polarised capacitors: must fit correct way round (electrolytic)
  • Inductance increases: more turns, closer turns, ferrite core
  • Capacitor: blocks DC, passes AC
  • Transformer: AC only; turns ratio = voltage ratio
  • Resonance: series circuit = minimum impedance; parallel = maximum
  • RMS: the DC equivalent heating value; V_rms = 0.707 × V_peak
  • Decibels: +3 dB doubles power; +10 dB multiplies by 10
  • Nyquist: sample rate ≥ twice the highest signal frequency
  • FET advantages at RX input: lower noise, higher input impedance
  • Varicap: capacitance controlled by reverse bias voltage
  • Class C: most efficient RF amplifier class; uses tuned output
  • SMPS: high internal frequency → smaller, lighter transformer
  • Battery Ah: current × time; choose size above calculated need
