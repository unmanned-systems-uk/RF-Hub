This short but exam-dense chapter underpins Software Defined Radio (Ch 10) and the DDS synthesiser in Ch 7. It introduces digital signal processing theory — how analogue signals are converted to numbers, sampled, transformed between domains, and reconstructed.

6A

Analogue and Digital Signals

An analogue signal can take any voltage or current value within the circuit’s limits and can contain any range of frequencies. Any noise added to it becomes indistinguishable from the wanted signal and degrades the information.

A digital signal is limited to a fixed number of discrete levels — most commonly two: logic ‘1’ and logic ‘0’. The actual voltages depend on the technology (RS232 uses ±3–25 V; microcontrollers often 0/3.3 V). Crucially, provided a voltage is within the specified range for its level, noise that does not push it over the threshold has no effect on the stored information. This is the fundamental advantage of digital representation over analogue.

ℹ
Key contrast: An analogue signal degrades continuously with noise. A digital signal is immune to noise up to the point where the noise margin is exceeded — then it fails catastrophically. This threshold behaviour makes digital systems far more robust in noisy environments.
6B

Binary Numbers

Microprocessors process information as ones and zeros — base 2 (binary). Each digit position doubles in value, so a 4-bit word can represent 0 to 15 (24 − 1 = 15).

An 8-bit byte covers 0–255 (28 − 1). The more bits used, the finer the representation.

Converting decimal to binary: repeatedly divide by 2 and record the remainders from bottom to top. For example, 13 → 8+4+1 → 1101.

Exam tip: Know that an N-bit ADC gives 2N levels. A 4-bit ADC = 16 levels; 8-bit = 256 levels; 14-bit = 16,384 levels. More bits = finer amplitude resolution = lower quantisation noise.
6C

Analogue to Digital Conversion (ADC)

To process an analogue signal digitally, it must be sampled at intervals and each sample represented as a binary number (a digital word).

Quantisation and Quantisation Noise

Because the amplitude is rounded to the nearest available digital level, the result is not exact — the error is called quantisation noise. With a 4-bit ADC, only 16 levels are available; a 14-bit ADC gives 16,384 levels.

Handling negative voltages: the zero-crossing is mapped to the mid-scale binary word (e.g. 1000 for a 4-bit ADC). Words below 1000 represent negative voltages; words above represent positive voltages.

SNR and Bit Depth

A 14-bit ADC gives a signal-to-quantisation-noise ratio (SNR) of better than 80 dB when the signal fully uses the ADC range. In practice, if the wanted signal is 60 dB below the ADC’s maximum, the effective SNR drops to 80 − 60 = 20 dB. The ADC design must ensure quantisation noise stays below the RF noise floor so the converter is not the limiting factor in receiving weak signals.

Exam note: The exact bits-to-SNR formula is not in the syllabus — the concept and approximate figures are what matter. Know that more bits = better SNR.
6D

Sampling Rate and the Nyquist Theorem

Sampling too infrequently misses detail; sampling too often creates excess data that overwhelms processing. Harry Nyquist proved the optimum:

ℹ
Nyquist theorem: A signal must be sampled at at least twice the highest frequency present to be faithfully reproduced.

If the highest audio frequency is 3 kHz, the minimum sample rate is 6 ksamples/s. In practice, sample rates are higher because real filters are imperfect.

Aliasing

If a frequency above half the sample rate is present during sampling, it is indistinguishable from a lower frequency — its alias. For example, with a 2 ksamples/s rate, a 1.1 kHz signal aliases to 900 Hz (the signal is 100 Hz above the Nyquist limit of 1 kHz; the alias appears 100 Hz below that limit).

Anti-aliasing filter: a low-pass filter fitted before the ADC removes any frequencies above the Nyquist limit before sampling, preventing aliasing. It is an essential part of every practical ADC system.

ℹ
Anti-aliasing filter is placed before the ADC input. It limits the signal bandwidth to below half the sample rate. Without it, high-frequency signals fold into the audio band as inaudible-to-visible artefacts.
6E

Fourier Transforms and the Frequency Domain

An analogue waveform displayed on an oscilloscope shows amplitude vs time — the time domain. The same signal can be represented as amplitude vs frequency — the frequency domain — showing which frequencies are present and at what level, exactly as a spectrum analyser displays.

The Fourier transform is the mathematical process that converts time-domain sample data into frequency-domain data. The Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) is a computationally efficient algorithm that allows near-real-time frequency analysis — the basis of the waterfall display in SDR software.

Time domainAmplitude vs timeOscilloscope
Frequency domainAmplitude vs frequencySpectrum analyser / SDR waterfall

The same signal — two different views. Neither is more “real”; each reveals information the other obscures.

ℹ
FFT is the algorithm that converts time-domain digital samples into a frequency-domain plot. SDR software uses the FFT continuously to generate the waterfall display in near-real-time. The spectrum analyser in the lab does the same thing in hardware.
6F

Digital to Analogue Conversion (DAC)

The reverse process reconstructs an analogue signal from digital words. A DAC reads a sequence of digital values and produces a corresponding analogue voltage. The output is a staircase approximating the desired waveform.

Look-Up Table Approach

A sinewave look-up table stores one cycle of a sine wave as digital amplitudes. Stepping through this table at a clock-controlled rate generates a sine wave whose frequency equals:

fout = (step rate through table) × (clock frequency / table length)

This is the basis of Direct Digital Synthesis (DDS) — covered in §7D.

Output Artefacts

The staircase output contains harmonics from the discrete amplitude levels. These must be removed by an analogue low-pass filter on the DAC output. Any errors in amplitude or timing also produce phase noise sidebands close to the wanted frequency — a limitation of DDS that cannot be filtered away.

ℹ
Phase noise in DDS arises from quantisation errors in the amplitude table and the finite step size. It manifests as elevated noise sidebands close to the carrier — close enough that the output LPF cannot remove them. This is a fundamental DDS limitation compared to a well-designed PLL synthesiser.

Self-Check — Chapter 6

Q1. Why is a digital signal more noise-resistant than an analogue signal?

A digital signal uses discrete levels (typically just two). Noise must exceed the threshold margin before it changes the logical value — any noise below the threshold has zero effect. An analogue signal carries any noise directly as distortion of the wanted information, with no immunity whatsoever.

Q2. An ADC has 8 bits. How many discrete amplitude levels can it represent?

28 = 256 levels (0 to 255).

Q3. State the Nyquist theorem in plain language.

A signal must be sampled at least twice as fast as the highest frequency present in order to be accurately reconstructed from the samples.

Q4. A system samples at 8 ksamples/s. What is the highest audio frequency that can be correctly represented, and what must be done to frequencies above this before sampling?

The Nyquist limit is 8 000 ÷ 2 = 4 kHz. Any frequencies above 4 kHz must be removed by an anti-aliasing low-pass filter placed before the ADC; otherwise they will alias to lower frequencies and corrupt the audio.

Q5. What is the difference between the time domain and the frequency domain view of a signal?

The time domain shows how the signal amplitude varies with time — the oscilloscope view. The frequency domain shows which frequencies are present and at what amplitude — the spectrum analyser or SDR waterfall view. The Fourier transform (FFT) converts between the two representations.

Q6. Why does the DAC output require a low-pass filter?

The DAC output is a staircase waveform containing harmonics generated by the discrete amplitude steps. The low-pass filter removes these harmonics, leaving only the wanted analogue waveform.

Chapter 6 — Key Facts Summary

  • Digital vs analogue: digital signals are immune to noise up to the threshold margin; analogue degrades continuously.
  • N-bit ADC gives 2N amplitude levels; 8-bit = 256; 14-bit = 16,384.
  • Quantisation noise = rounding error from mapping analogue amplitude to nearest digital level.
  • 14-bit ADC SNR ≈ 80 dB when signal uses full ADC range.
  • Nyquist theorem: sample rate must be ≥ 2 × highest signal frequency.
  • Aliasing occurs when signal frequency exceeds half the sample rate; the signal folds to a false lower frequency.
  • Anti-aliasing filter (LPF before ADC) prevents aliasing — mandatory in practical systems.
  • Time domain = amplitude vs time (oscilloscope). Frequency domain = amplitude vs frequency (spectrum analyser / waterfall).
  • FFT (Fast Fourier Transform) converts digital time-domain samples to frequency-domain display in near-real-time.
  • DAC produces a staircase output; output LPF removes harmonic content from the steps.
  • DDS uses a look-up table + DAC + LPF to synthesise frequencies digitally. Produces phase noise that LPF cannot remove.
